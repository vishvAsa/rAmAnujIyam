Stories from Chennai and Beyond

These oral histories emerged from the heart of Chennai, its surrounding suburbs, nearby towns such as Sriperumbudur, and villages between the hubs. They represent several generations—from ‘seniors’ to students— many castes, and both men and women. Each story expresses a lived experience, which is the raw material from which narratives are fashioned.

The Grandson and the Outcaste Hamlet

The old Ambassador car lurched through yet another pothole. The monsoon had reminded the city once again that natural forces can halt human progress. The pock-marked road wound to the northwestern edge of Chennai’s sprawling suburbs, relentlessly intruding on the paddy fields. Endless storefronts stacked with stainless-steel pots, tires or saris—this is the land of the small businessman—made it impossible to know where one municipality ended and another began. Occasionally, our driver stopped for directions. In a staccato voice, someone listed the turns and gesticulated toward the horizon. Again, we bumped along for what must have been an hour in heavy traffic. As we neared our destination1 and entered a maze of alleys, we stopped for instructions more often.

And then, miraculously, we were there in what had once been a paṟaiccēri (outcaste hamlet),2 now called Ekangipuram, adjacent to a huge cloth mill, the oldest and largest in the area near a major train station. Suburbanisation had engulfed the area with roads, schools and hospitals. Land in this once-despised hamlet was fetching a good price and many owners were selling out. Some Dalits had moved into suburban neighborhoods with a mix of castes, hoping to hide their identity and take advantage of new opportunities. Others took what little they had into the city core to start new lives, only to find themselves stuck in slums, their search for justice ending in the terrifying struggle for survival.

A man directed us down a tiny alley, almost impassable because of motorcycles parked on the edge. At that moment, something caught our attention: a Śrīvaiṣṇava emblem on a house wall, the ubiquitous conch, nāmam and discus3 that adorn, sanctify and protect place or person. We had arrived. Our host ran forward to greet us, bowing with hands folded, uttering vaṇakkam (welcome).

This handsome young man is J. N. B. Chakravarthi Ramanuja Dasar (Chakra for short). In his household also live his wife Mahalakshmi (who is pregnant with their first child; she had a son later that year), his mother Lokanayaki, and his maternal grandmother Kumbhavalai Nacciyar. Chakra works in an automobile engineering business on Mount Road in Chennai. Several years later, he worked in the accounts department of Surya TV. Mahalakshmi has a B.Com. degree. She is a medical transcriptionist. The transcripts are sent from London and the United States; when they arrive in oral form, she writes them into formal medical records on the computer, and then she sends the computer files back.

The family’s religious identity has been defined by formal initiation (samāśrayaṇam) into Śrīvaiṣṇavism. The male members of the family had been the first to be initiated. Their names include the initiation name Ramanuja Dasar. According to Mahalakshmi, the women were initiated only after marriage. [[P95]]

When Chakra’s mother, Lokanayaki, married, she did this ritual and began to worship Perumāḷ whom her husband’s family considered to be the supreme god.4 And then, so did her mother and her father but not her brother. My own family worshipped Perumāḷ, although only I have had initiation. This was after my marriage to Chakra. A Brahmin ācārya initiated us.

We entered their home. A large house-shrine dominates the main room. Chakra prostrated himself before it and then honoured K. K. A. Venkatachari and me with ceremonial shawls. Photos of deities include Vēṅkaṭeśvara, Krishna, Rāma, Varadarāja, Perumāḷ, and also Āṇṭāḷ, the female poetess who is also considered a goddess, the consort of Lord Raṅganātha. There is a small image of Rāmānuja, too. Chakra said, ‘The family does daily pūjā, chanting together short poetic works of the āḻvārs5 or a selection of Nammāḻvār’s verses.’6 I asked about other rituals that require the chanting of āḻvār hymns. ‘We chant some at weddings,’ Chakra said, ‘and during a wife’s pregnancy.’7

My attention turned to the wall next to that shrine. As in many Hindu household shrines, it displays pictures of earlier generations: deceased parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles. The conversation focussed on a picture of one of Chakra’s grandfathers, J. J. Thirunarayana Ramanuja Dasar (1901–83), a very distinguished looking man.

Thirunarayana, as I will call him here, came from a poor family. Chakra told me the following: ‘My grandparents8 had a piece of agricultural land near Vellore, in a village called Kilamanavur. The village had a small Krishna temple. My great grandfather, Jaganathan, taught my grandfather with the help of local pandits.’

I later discovered that one Hindu non-Brahmin ascetic (ekāṅgi) from Srirangam had lectured in Vellore. He was probably (Kōyiḻ) Ekāṅgi Svāmikaḷ, whom I discussed in Chapter 1 and discuss in this chapter as well. The area of [[P96]] Vellore produced considerable Dalit mobilisation. Vellore had absorbed Christian influence too, which explains its famous Christian Medical College and the fact that many Dalits had converted to Christianity. Other Dalits had turned to neo-Buddhism. Krishnaswamy (1916–73), for instance, studied in Madras, then returned to Vellore and, as a follower of Ambedkar’s neo-Buddhism, worked his entire life to improve Dalit well-being.9

Chakra told me more about the family’s history in Chennai.

My grandfather married Godai Nacciyar. They had three sons and two daughters.10 The family lived outside Chennai in this outcaste hamlet.11 My grandfather had a good job. He was employed as a diesel-engine examiner [[P97]] for the southern railway; his second son, my uncle J. N. Devaperumal Ramanuja Dasar, was a manager for the southern railway.

The British often argued that the railway, which they gradually introduced after 1853, would usher in capitalist modernity; it would create a work ethic, moreover, and dissolve caste. Even Marx claimed that it would bring progress to India by eliminating the hereditary divisions of labour. Nevertheless, it took over a century to achieve some progress. Dissolving caste was not part of this. In the meantime, railways created new hierarchies with Europeans and Eurasians at the top, Brahmins in choice ‘native positions’, such as stationmaster, and other castes in lower jobs, such as ironworkers. The obvious contradiction between the British rhetoric on equality and this workplace inequality12—status, pay, access to education, and other opportunities—contributed to agitation by Indian railway employees. By the 1920s, with a growing number of strikes, railway workers were becoming a dangerous force both for the Raj, which needed trains to transport goods and soldiers, and for Indian political parties.

For instance, to be successful, the Congress Party had to compete with the Communist Party and other parties for the loyalty of these railway workers who daily experienced indignity. Mahatma Gandhi visited railway unions in order to win their support for Indian nationalism in general and for the Congress Party in particular. Many railway workers became Congress Party stalwarts, although they remained potential threats to any party in power even after Independence. Gradually, however, fair wages, good working conditions and democratic principles prevailed.

With that in mind, let us return to the interview. The conversation turned to the family’s political affiliations. Chakra informed me about his family’s connection with the Congress Party.

My grandfather was a staunch member of the Congress party, and his family had personal connections with Mahatma Gandhi. When Gandhi came to Chennai, he stayed with a Śrīvaiṣṇava family. The couple named [[P98]] their daughter after Gandhi’s wife Kasturba. Later, this daughter became the wife of J. N. Arangaram Ramanuja Dasar, my uncle. The Congress connection has continued into my generation.

Thirunarayana’s job in the railways and his political connection to Gandhi might have been related to Gandhi’s interest in winning support from railway workers. Now I understood the presence of a three-foot statue of Gandhi in Chakra’s home.

Chakra made it clear that his family has had additional political connections:

My grandfather was close friends with Kamaraj. Known for his honesty, Kamaraj worked in the Congress party and went from the lowest level to the highest. He was from the Nadar caste, which was prevalent in Ramanathapuram, the southernmost district of Tamil Nadu. Nadars were traditionally toddy tappers.13 Their status was not high; they used to fight with outcastes over who had the right to pull the festival chariot (on which the deity rides) through the streets surrounding the temple. Kamaraj became the Tamil Nadu Congress president and later president of the National Congress Party. He had close relations with Vaiṣṇavas because Nadar religion has some Vaiṣṇava roots (see Appendix 6), but he also had good relations with all religions.

C. Rajagopalachari—a Brahmin Śrīvaiṣṇava whose daughter had married Mahatma Gandhi’s son—had been Chief Minister in Tamil Nadu, Governor of Bengal and the Governor-General of India. Then he became Chief Minister in Tamil Nadu for a second time. People thought that with his admirable statesmanship and good governance, he was the only one who could handle the DMK, which was becoming powerful. Kamaraj and other Congress non-Brahmins protested his appointment on the basis that he was a Brahmin. As a result, C. Rajagopalachari resigned as Chief Minister, and Kamaraj became Chief Minister in 1954. [[P99]]

The family showed me a picture of Indira Gandhi with Kamaraj, who had just died and was lying in state (1975). Thirunarayana was there (even though he could barely be seen at the edge of the picture). ‘Ekangipuram still has some Congress party influence,’ Chakra said, ‘even though the DMK and AIDMK are also active in this area.’

Chakra remarked that his grandfather Thirunarayana was also known as a very learned Śrīvaiṣṇava. ‘My grandfather was called a nityasūri (an eternal being) because of his exemplary behaviour, and an ubhayavedāntin because of his knowledge of both Tamil and Sanskrit scripture. He established a Vedic school where he and then later his sons taught Vedic hymns.’

As an ubhayavedāntin, Thirunarayana would have known Tamil and some Sanskrit. There is another example of this phenomenon: members of a Dalit community learning and chanting Vedic hymns. Vasudha Narayanan describes one of these communities in Melkote. This group recites selections from the Vedas, including the Puruṣasūkta, and the Taittirīya Upaniṣad. I was told by others that Thirunarayana was in communication with this Melkote community, perhaps because the Melkote community’s spiritual advisor was Sri Govinda Ramanuja Dasar, who was from Ganesapuram in Madras,14 and Thirunarayana knew him. Aside from his religious knowledge, Thirunarayana knew several languages. His sons were also known for their linguistic skills: the first son was a scholar in Tamil, the second was good in English, and the third was proficient in Hindi.15

Thirunarayana was a leader in his neighbourhood. According to Chakra, government documents show that Thirunarayana and one of his three sons fought with the municipality to change the name of the locale from the pejorative paṟaiccēri (outcaste hamlet) to Ekangipuram. After all, it contained the samādhi of (Kōyiḻ) Ekāṅgi Svāmikaḷ, whom we have already encountered.16 Perhaps because Ekāṅgi Swāmikaḷ gave lectures and initiations in this paṟaiccēri to Dalits, he was buried here. [[P100]]

When the American scholar Raymond Williams17 visited Ekangipuram in 1991, he interviewed two of Thirunarayana’s sons (who have since died). They told him that there were 15 Śrīvaiṣṇava Harijan families living there. They were strictly vegetarian, and, like orthodox Brahmins, did not even eat onions or garlic. They led bhajanas (devotional songs). One brother did the rituals for new temples in the area. The family, Williams wrote, took care of the burial place of the Ekāṅgi and his shrine in the adjacent building.

On the day of his visit, Williams witnessed a procession of Śrīvaiṣṇavas at Ekangipuram. The people who gathered at the shrine were almost all Dalits, except for a few Brahmin visitors. They went through the colony with a group chanting some āḻvār verses at the beginning of the procession (the lead person was holding a copy of the Divyaprabandham, the Tamil Śrīvaiṣṇava scripture), and another group chanting bhajanas at the end of the procession and dancing. That day, the procession had two drummers, a person who played the cymbals and a harmonium player. The procession stopped at many houses and received honours: sacred water, sandal paste and kumkum on the feet. Women of the households prostrated themselves before those in the procession. By the time they returned to the samādhi, the group had grown to about 100 people. There was more chanting and dancing, followed by a lecture by a Brahmin about Āṇṭāḷ.

Now back to the present. As we walked to the samādhi a short distance away, Chakra said,

Ekāṅgi Svāmikaḷ’s disciple Appaṉ cared for the samādhi after his death. After Appaṉ’s own expiry, his picture was also hung in the shrine, near his master’s. My grandfather and his family lived in a building adjacent to the samādhi, caring for the shrine and tending to the daily worship of the images. Members of my family continue to do this.’18

When we returned to Ekangipuram, a few weeks later, Śrīvaiṣṇavas had gathered in Chakra’s house for our visit. Chakra commented on the changes in this neighbourhood. [[P101]]

Previously, there were 25 to 30 outcaste Śrīvaiṣṇavas in the vicinity; now there is only my family. Once there was an Āṇṭāḷ association in this area; now it is no longer functioning. By contrast, Christianity is growing in popularity. For this reason, we are trying to revive Śrīvaiṣṇavism in the neighbourhood. In 2005, we started a new organisation called Varadaraja Sabhai. Ten people attended; six belonged to my family. We also periodically hold functions and invite honoured guests to speak about Śrīvaiṣṇavism. One, for instance, was when the family offered a stone for building the temple at Ayodhyā in 1987.19 Another was when two honoured guests— Varada Ethiraja Jeeyar Swami20 and Kumaravadi Swamikal—gave talks, which we recorded on video.

The honoured guest on this occasion was K. K. A. Venkatachari. His homily was on Nammāḻvār’s Tiruvāymoḻi 3.7.9.

Those whose minds are absorbed [in the Lord] [thinking] [we are] ‘the servants of the King who holds a powerful discus, the One of sapphire-hue,’
even though they be outcastes of the outcastes who have no goodness
are inferior to the four castes that support the families,
the servants of their servants [are] our masters. (Nammāḻvār, Tiruvāymoḻi 3.7.9; Young [trans.])

kulaṁ tāṅku cātikaḷ nāliluṁ kīḻ iḻintu
ettaṉai nalam tāṉ ilāta caṇṭāḷa caṇṭāḷarkaḷākiluṁ
valam tāṅku cakkarattu aṇṇal maṇi vaṇṇaṟku āḷ eṉṟu
uḷ kalantār aṭiyār taṁ aṭiyār eṁ aṭikaḷē.21

This verse was apropos, given its focus on outcastes (here, the word is caṇṭāḷa) who are absorbed [in God], and even though they are the lowest of the low, inferior to the four castes (cāti; jāti)—that is, the four varṇas—that support the families on which society depends, they are our true masters. The implication is that even the Brahmins should serve them with humility. After reciting the verse, Venkatachari elucidated its meaning, drawing from commentaries by ācāryas between the thirteenth [[P102]] and fifteenth century. Their basic point is that outcastes are pre-eminently qualified to serve God, because their essential nature is to serve others and therefore they exemplify the ideal of service, which others should imitate. As such, outcastes are the real masters or preceptors. Service to God and his devotees (kaiṅkarya) is, of course, a key concept of Śrīvaiṣṇavism. As the homily drew to a close, Chakra presented ceremonial shawls to Venkatachari and me, and performed pūjā. Then we examined the many family pictures.

At the end of our visit, we walked to Ekāṅgi Svāmikaḷ’s samādhi. I learned that there is now a legal dispute.22 Someone had seized control of the property, which had become valuable as part of metropolitan Chennai. Several weeks later, I returned to Chakra’s and found that the entire samādhi had been bulldozed. One chapter of local history had disappeared. Now, not only was Chakra’s family almost the only Śrīvaiṣṇava one left in the area, but there was also no trace of Ekāṅgi Swāmikaḷ. [[P103]]

The Granddaughter and Rāmānuja’s House

N. D. Sabari Natchi is Chakra’s first cousin and the granddaughter of J. J. Thirunarayana Ramanuja Dasar. Her father23 followed in her grandfather’s footsteps, working for the southern railways, and eventually became a manager; her mother24 was a teacher for the Corporation of Madras. One of her brothers is a mechanical engineer and the other is a civil engineer. Her husband Sivagnanan is director of a Central Government department that promotes small enterprises throughout Tamil Nadu and Puducherry. Sabari Natchi works for the Central Government office of the Oil and Natural Gas Corporation as a purchase officer.

I first met Sabari Natchi and her husband at K. K. A. Venkatachari’s house in Triplicane. It was the Tamil month of Mārkaḻi (December–January) when Śrīvaiṣṇava Brahmins meet in the early morning to chant Āṇṭāḷ’s Tiruppāvai, 30 verses about maidens gathering in the early morning to go to the temple to perform a vow.25 Ostensibly, the maidens do so to find good husbands. On a deeper level, though, they do so to serve the Lord. Sabari Natchi and her husband were invited guests on this occasion. After the recitation, Venkatachari introduced them to me. They invited us to visit them the following weekend.

And there we went. Another long sojourn took us to the even more distant northwestern suburbs of Chennai, this time to a place named Athipet, part of Ambattur. As we neared our destination, the roads were so muddy that we almost gave up for fear that the old car would get stuck. We finally came to Mahatma Gandhi Road and an open field with a palatial new house. Checking the address, we realised that we had reached our destination. As we climbed out of the car, we saw the words ‘Rāmānujar Illam’ (Rāmānuja’s House) on the façade. And there were Sabari Nacchi, Sivagnanam and their two sons: S. Nedumaalavan and S. Shree Saranathaperumal. They greeted us and offered us ceremonial shawls.

Our conversation began with the story of Sabari Natchi’s life. ‘Until marriage,’ she said, ‘I lived in Ekangipuram. My brother still lives there. My family could not find a suitable Śrīvaiṣṇava match for me, and so they turned to a Śaiva family that worships Murukaṉ.’ Sivagnanam chimed in: ‘I made it to the short list and promised to follow Śrīvaiṣṇavism and be a strict vegetarian. When I had samāśrayaṇam, I was renamed Srinivasin, but I don’t use that name except within the family.’

As we sat down after tea, I asked them: ‘What is the best aspect of Śrīvaiṣṇavism?’ They eagerly responded.

Sabari Natchi: ‘We are very fond of Rāmānuja. He rejected untouchability; no one is big or small. There is equality. This allows us to live with satisfaction and in a decent way, which is sometimes difficult to do in our community.’

Sivagnanam: ‘In the Epics, Krishna describes how to follow values and morals. We find these in Rāmānuja who says, “I can see the Lord in the form of lower caste people.” I am impressed, moreover, that Rāmānuja revealed the mantra om, namo nārāyaṇāya to everyone.’26 [[P104]]

(Sabari Natchi, too, brought up this point later in the conversation.)

Nedumaalavan: ‘Once, when Rāmānuja went for his sacred bath, he went with an upper-caste person and returned with a lower caste person.’

Sivagnanam: ‘If you just whisper the name of God with full devotion, you become a devotee and get what you desire.’

Sabari Natchi: ‘Vaiṣṇavas are very strict. The religion helps them to have discipline and get ahead.’

Sivagnanam: ‘Dr Venkatachari understands real Vaiṣṇavism. He is very devout. Devotion has been nullified by northerners and others who claim to be true Śrīvaiṣṇavas. By just wearing the nāmam without having the morals and good knowledge, discrimination comes in. Therefore, lower castes have to be empowered with knowledge and empowered economically. Education is a must, and we must be educated with values. We are not interested in big rituals, only just the values. The number of Śrīvaiṣṇava Harijans is growing; however, many are ignorant. It would be good if upper castes teach lower castes. The lower castes will listen. Upper castes should take this seriously. In the Gītā, Krishna spread the message to everyone. The upper castes don’t want to do this. All people are equal; they come without anything into this world and won’t take anything with them after death. Naidus and Reddiyars, too, maintain a distance from us. Because of this, we need piety, vegetarianism and ethics. This makes humans full. Śrīvaiṣṇava Brahmins should come forward and educate lower castes in order to transform them into Śrīvaiṣṇavas, although they need to reeducate the Brahmins and caste Hindus too.’

Sabari Natchi: ‘Every day, I tell stories about Śrīvaiṣṇavism to my sons. I use the Tattva Loka, a monthly magazine,27 and buy Śrīvaiṣṇava books. Our sons go to an Arya Samaj school, which is near our home. They do pūjā and yajña rituals there. Both kids want to do Śrīvaiṣṇava service.’

Sabari Natchi: ‘There is a lot of politics in the temples. The [[P105]] most important thing is being a good human being and only then a Vaiṣṇava. One needs the real culture of being a Śrīvaiṣṇava. Besides K. K. A. Venkatachari, another real Śrīvaiṣṇava is Varataa Ethiraja Jeeyar Swami28 of Sriperumbudur. We know him well and always get his blessing when we go there.’

Sabari Natchi and Sivagnanam mentioned that they rarely went to Śrīvaiṣṇava lectures, yet did so whenever they had the time. They are intent, however, on doing kaiṅkarya. ‘The whole family,’ said Sabari Natchi,

goes to Tirupati for the Brahmotsavam festival. Following a tradition started by my father, we distribute beforehand an invitation to poor Śrīvaiṣṇavas. It says, ‘If you go to Tirupati, we will feed you. We will also pay for the train and for darshan [sight of and being seen by God].’ We spend a week there, cooking and offering food to the devotees at the Tirumalai-Tirupati Devasthanam guesthouse.

Despite successful careers, a happy home, and deep Śrīvaiṣṇava identity, the family occasionally encounters the old outcaste stigma. Sabari Natchi and her sons, for instance, have not yet had samāśrayaṇam. Nevertheless, they want it. Sabari Natchi explained:

When my grandfather was ill, he told his first son to get initiation from the same ācārya who had performed the initiation for him. Now that ācārya’s son is doing the initiations. However, when I went to him, along with others from my community, he refused to do it. I want to follow the instructions of my grandfather and have an ācārya from this family perform the initiation. I want to chant from this lineage during the pūjā, moreover, in the traditional way. I do not want to deviate from the family tradition.

Sivagnanam remarked, ‘Go to the person who is willing to do the initiation. When driven away, why follow the forefather? Your grandfather would not go to such an ācārya if he were alive today!’ With the interview concluding, we went on a tour [[P106]] of their new home, ‘Rāmānuja’s house’, including its shrine room with its contemporary pictures of the deities.

For centuries, the entry of Dalits into major Śrīvaiṣṇava temples had been contentious, as the following story of Tiruppāṇāḻvār reveals:

One day a minstrel, duly aware of his outcaste status at the temple, was singing to the Lord from outside the Srirangam temple. A priest who was fetching water from the Kaveri for the ritual tried to chase him away from the river to prevent him from polluting it. The Lord himself intervened and chastised the priest in a dream for his behavior. The next day, the priest himself carried Tiruppāṇ on his shoulder into the temple’s sanctum. On seeing the Lord, the āḻvār sang the ten verses known as the Amalanātippirāṉ.29

This story of Tiruppāṇ reveals the desire of some Brahmins in the past to keep outcastes outside the temples. The command of God was to bring them inside. This might well reflect a growing tension in the community at that time, between Brahmanical views on orthodox purity (which considered outcastes impure and therefore not allowed in the sacred temple space) and the inclusive Śrīvaiṣṇava theology (which argued that any devotee of God is to be accepted and honoured). The orthodox eventually won; outcastes could not enter the major temples until the mid-twentieth century.

Sriperumbudur, the birthplace of Rāmānuja, also excluded outcastes from the temple. For this reason, the bronze image of Rāmānuja was taken in procession around the temple in the Tamil month of Tai (mid-January to mid-February), during a three-day festival at Sriperumbudur, which commemorates the installation of Rāmānuja’s image there.30 Śrīvaiṣṇavas have believed, after all, that Rāmānuja is both divine and the mediator between the Supreme God and humans; by surrendering to an ācārya or jīyar, they are in point of fact surrendering to Rāmānuja himself, which guarantees salvation.

During this festival, outcastes had the right to carry the palanquin. Just as the gracious deity went outside his home or [[P107]] temple on special occasions, so that everyone could see him, and he could proffer the opportunity for service, devotion and salvation, so did Rāmānuja. He had (and still has) an iconic body. The Lord going in procession outside the temple, which was common in most temples, was important, because even though outcastes could not go inside the temple proper, they could perform service outside the temple and have darśana of God there. With Dalit political mobilisation in the twentieth century, Dalits no longer accepted their exclusion from the temple and relegation of their darśana to the streets. Nonetheless, they have maintained the tradition of carrying the palanquin as an honour that belongs to their community.

‘My grandfather was instrumental,’ said Sabari Natchi, ‘in improving the relation of the Harijans with Śrī Rāmānuja’s temple authorities in Sriperumbudur and agitated for their right to enter the temple.’ In this activity, I might add, Thirunarayana could have been inspired by Gandhi’s visit to Vellore31 and Pallikonda. When Gandhi visited a temple in Pallikonda in 1934, he was told that the outcastes who had experienced severe discrimination in the area were not allowed to enter. Gandhi declared that he would not enter the temple until the outcastes too could go inside. Caste Hindus denied his request, and so Gandhi went away.32

Gandhi agitated for temple entry, not only at this temple but also throughout India, including many places in Tamil Nadu. Thirunarayana had political and family connections with the Mahatma, and if Thirunarayana had been at this attempted temple entry, or at least heard about it or about one of the many other events when Gandhi agitated for temple entry in Tamil Nadu, he might have been inspired to work for the opening of the Sriperumbudur temple to Harijans. According to Sabari Natchi:

my grandfather had bought land at Sriperumbudur and built a maṭha there in 1936, calling it Srimatha Yamunariya Priyar Bhakti Jana Sabha. Since 1939, outcastes have gathered there to celebrate the birthday of Śrī Rāmānuja for ten days in April–May, because Śrī Rāmānuja had [[P108]] shown the path to many people. This maṭha is meant only for Harijans. It provides food and shelter during festivals. My grandfather preached to Harijans at that time about Śrīvaiṣṇavism and told everyone how to do pūjā. After his death, his first son did this.

The government finally granted Dalits temple entry in 1947.33 It took some time, however, for temples to comply, and even now there are problems sometimes.

Thirunarayana’s special connection to Sriperumbudur might be related to his (and his family’s) samāśrayaṇam by Sāttikvaācārya from nearby Tirumalicai, one of the few Brahmin ācāryas who initiated outcastes at that time (I discuss him in Chapter 6). In 2009, Sabari Natchi described to me the plans to celebrate the fifty-second year of the maṭha:

Five hundred to six hundred Harijans from all over south India will come to this. Funds are collected from the devotees to finance the event. Now we are renovating the maṭha at Sriperumbudur, and I am in charge. I continue to do the service of my father and grandfather. I teach people how to do the pūjās, bhajanas and so forth when Harijans come from all over south India.

Obviously, Sriperumbudur has been a hub of Dalit Śrīvaiṣṇavism since Thirunarayana’s time, if not earlier. [[P109]]

The ‘almost ācārya’ and his Cousins

My destination was an ashram at Punga Medu, in the town of Minjur located northwest of the huge Ennore port north of Chennai. The road-trip there was an adventure, both because of monsoon damage to the roads and because of the many huge lorries that were parked bumper to bumper in Ennore, awaiting their turn to be loaded, and making the road virtually one-way. Because the trucks had no drivers and therefore seemed to be parked for future action, our driver, uttering a prayer to Lord Vēṅkaṭeśvara, took off down the single lane for several kilometres. I held my breath, hoping that we would not meet an oncoming lorry and have to head for a deep ditch at the side of the road. We made it through and soon came to a village surrounded by verdant fields and then the ashram.

[[P110]] I was about to meet a Telugu family that migrated into this area several generations ago.34 The grandfather was a farmer who had about 40 acres of land (although only four remain). He did the paddy measurements and solved problems for the village council. ‘A very attractive man,’ said his grandson Padu, ‘he was famous as a singer. One day, when he was husking rice, he looked to the sky and sang. It began to rain everywhere except at that spot. Because this was miraculous, he built a temple there. That was about twenty-five years ago.’

Today, this temple has been enlarged and is part of an ashram that is run by the family, which has been Vaiṣṇava for several generations. The key person is Sudarshana ‘Acharya’, who was forty-two when I first interviewed him.

Besides Sudarshana, I interviewed two of his cousins: M. Dhamodharan, a lawyer at the High Court in Chennai who often returns to his home village, and Padu, who lives with his family next to the ashram.

Sudarshana’s Saga

My family is Vaiṣṇava, and so I went with them as a child to Tirumalai-Tirupati. When I was in the seventh grade, I became interested in social work and the religious path of devotion. At that time, I saw in a dream a figure with nāmam, crown, flowers and jewels. The next month, when I went to Tirupati, I recognised that same figure as Lord Bālāji (Vēṅkaṭeśvara). Bālāji encouraged me to pursue the way of devotion.

When I was in the tenth grade, I saw Tirupati Bālāji again in a dream. He told me to learn more about the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition. I began by contacting Varataa Ethiraja Jeeyar Swami of Sriperumbudur, who asked me to go to the Śaṅkara maṭha in Kanchipuram for training. Between 1983 and 1986, I studied yoga, siddha, āyurveda and naturopathy, as well as how to read the Vedas and give lectures. I lived in Sriperumbudur during my studies. In 1986, at the age of eighteen, I was initiated by Ethiraja. At that time, he gave me a new name, Shriman Sudarshana Acharya. [[P111]]

‘ācārya’ is certainly a provocative title, because this role had remained under Brahmin control, notwithstanding the agitation of figures such as Ekāṅgi Svāmikaḷ and Bhuvanagiri in the previous century to open the role to Bhāgavatas. Something else was unusual here. Brahmin ācāryas marry and belong to one of the seventy-four families appointed, according to tradition, by Rāmānuja.35 Sudarshana, who was not married, obviously did not fit this description, and so, as he told me, he was an ‘almost ācārya’. Because of the unorthodoxy of his title, it came as no surprise to find that Sudarshana also used the Brahmin title jīyar. He showed some signs of being an ascetic. He wore an orange dhoti and rudrākṣa beads, for instance, or sometimes a white dhoti and orange upper-cloth. (Ascetics wear orange vestments and rudrākṣa beads.) His cousin Padu described Sudarshana as a celibate. When I asked Sudarshana if he was a saṃnyāsin (even though, traditionally, only Brahmins can be saṃnyāsins), he replied, ‘Ethiraja told me that I will have the position of being “almost” a saṃnyāsin. I am neither a saṃnyāsin nor a married householder, but in between.’

Sudarshana calls himself Chinna Jīyar (little Jīyar), too, in a pamphlet that he wrote. If Sudarshana is the little jīyar, who is the big one? Near Sudarshana’s office hangs a huge banner with three figures: at the top is Rāmānuja, in the middle Varataa Ethiraja Jeeyar, and at the bottom Sudarshana. Chakra and Sabari Natchi had already mentioned Ethiraja from Sriperumbudur. The banner makes it clear that he is Sudarshana’s ‘Big Jīyar’.36

Sudarshana continued with more stories about his life.

As a traditional (siddha) physician who uses no Western medicine, I treat people on Sundays and give medicines for free. This is one reason the place is so popular. About 100 people come. When I cure people, they want to offer something to me, and I tell them to offer a statue or something else for the temple. I am an astrologer, too. And I give books and pencils to poor students who worship at the temple. However, I don’t like doing rites of passage such as naming ceremonies, marriages and funerals, although I will bless marriages if I am invited [[P112]] to them and will do nārāyaṇabali [the ritual to counter the bad karma of an unusual death] and chant verses from the Tiruvāymoḻi after the death of a Vaiṣṇava. Sometimes, I perform Vedic fire rituals or homa at temple openings—sixty-three temples have been recently opened—and do blessings at new homes and school functions. And each year, I give samāśrayaṇam to about 1,000 people who come from nearby districts in both Tamil Nadu and Andhra. In one hour, they obtain new lives; they become pure vegetarians, wear the nāmam and do daily pūjā.

Sudarshana gave me a tour of the ashram. At the centre of the compound is a temple dedicated to God Śrīnivāsa, who is present as an immovable stone statue and a movable bronze festival image. On the left of the main temple is Sudarshana’s office. At the back of the ashram are cowsheds and the medical clinic. To the right of the main temple is a smaller temple. Dedicated to Rāmānuja, it contains both immovable and movable images. In addition, we find on the right side of the main temple another shrine with images representing the nine planets.

On another visit to the Minjur ashram, a few weeks later, I watched Sudarshana giving a woman samāśrayaṇam, branding her with the ritual utensils heated in a sacred fire, and revealing to her the secret mantras. I learned that her husband was waiting in a rickshaw on the street outside, too sick to come into the temple for the ritual. I saw Sudarshana initiate him there.

Padu told me that local people highly respect Sudarshana. Devotees support the temple with their gifts and locals as well as visitors from Chennai and elsewhere visit. Saturday night is the main pūjā. On this visit, I saw Sudharshana performing pūjā with some Vedic verses. He led bhajana singing, too, along with four young men dressed in lungis. Because they were bare-chested, I could see that they all wore sacred threads like those worn by Brahmins.37 A [[P113]] 94-year-old man was wandering about, touching the feet of devotees. When the bhajanas started, he began to hop and dance. After a while, he put on a Hanuman wooden mask and danced with it. As the tempo increased, he stuck out his tongue. Everyone knew that he had dementia and gently joked with him, yet also accepted him as a genuine devotee.

I noticed many visiting devotees. Some were merely Saturday visitors, whereas others were gathering for the biannual pilgrimage by foot to Tirupati, which is about 150 km away. Gradually, men who had been seated on the floor singing bhajanas stood up and a procession began. Leading it was a man with a small white horse. The four young bhajana leaders now carried the palanquin with the image of God Śrīnivāsa, swaying it back and forth. Then came Sudarshana, walking under a ceremonial parasol. Several young men pulled a big drum, which they beat. The procession moved slowly down a lane to the road. Then, the pilgrims moved on. Along the way, the group stops at various charitable guesthouses for the night. Sudarshana has a connection with a jīyar in Tirupati who had made the arrangements.

I visited Sudarshana’s ashram once more to witness the ritual wedding of Āṇṭāḷ (as goddess) and God. This was a major event with many visitors, processions, and a Vedic-style fire ritual. Sudarshana was the chief ritualist. Occasionally, he consulted a book, even though he knew most of the chants by heart. It was a grand celebration. As Sudarshana’s cousin Dhamodharan told me, ‘Sudarshana is doing pūjās beautifully. Even the Brahmins can’t do it like that.’ When interviewing Sudarshana, I mentioned rumours that Ethiraja might appoint him as his successor. He replied, ‘I have no idea who the successor will be. What God wants, he will do.’38 [[P114]]

M. Dhamodharan’s Life Story

I met Dhamodharan on several occasions, both at the ashram and in Chennai. Gradually, I learned his life story.

I was born and brought up in Minjur. It is an agricultural village. Both my father and mother were from farming families. The land where the ashram is located was once my family’s land. We gave it to the ashram. Then a devotee purchased some more land next door and gave it to the ashram.

I am the first one in my family to be educated. My first degree was in history: European, Indian and Tamil. I studied at Loganatha Narayanaswamy Government College in Ponneri and graduated in 1976. Then I went to the Law College of the University of Madras now called Dr. Ambedkar Law College. I joined in 1980 and graduated in 1983, after which I enrolled in the Bar Council as a lawyer. I obtained a certificate that allowed me to practice in all courts. I went to the high court. Now, I have a law office there with a typist. My specialisations are labour law, criminal law, employee problems within the government service and real estate. I am currently investigating twenty-five criminal cases of office fraud for the Ministry of Company Affairs, which regulates small businesses regarding problems of trading to relatives, inside deals, and so forth.

I belong to the Indian National Congress. I was the Vice President of its Legal Cell for Tamil Nadu. Through that, I developed many contacts in Tamil Nadu. I was a member of the Free Legal Aid Committee, a Tamil Nadu Government body. Now, I am a senior counsel for them. This is a free service for the poor. The Dalit Sahitiya Academy in Delhi gave me a national award for my service to the community. In my regular job, I have clients from Chennai, Ooty, Coimbatore, and elsewhere.

I have not had samāśrayaṇam because I like non-vegetarian food. I have a pūjā room in my home. In the morning, after I bathe and don a fresh dhoti, I stand before the photo of Lord Vēṅkateśvara (Bālāji), our family deity. I have not memorised Divyaprabandham verses; rather, I offer prayers from my heart.

My wife is a medical doctor. She also serves the poor. Our only child, a daughter, is now in a boarding school near Coimbatore. She wants to be a medical doctor like her mother and will soon take entrance exams. [[P115]]

We have land in Minjur. Our dream is to start a free medical college and hospital. However, we need money to start. We are looking for an NRI (a non-resident Indian) or someone like you to help.

Padu’s Saga

Another of Sudarshana’s cousins is Padu. He had quite a different perspective on religion.

I went to a government school in the village. Later, I went to the government sculpture school in Mahabalipuram, where I learned how to carve stone temple statues. I have not looked for that kind of work, although my brother who studied at the same school has carved some of the statues around the temple here and now acts as a priest assisting Sudarshana. As for me, I dream of having a jewellery shop or becoming an actor, playing villain roles in Tamil films.

He showed me some terrifying faces for his upcoming film audition, then continued:

I was initiated ten years ago. It was done by Sudarshana. My grandfather, father, brothers, and others had all had it. It didn’t really change my life. The real change was when I married at twenty-three and had children. There were more expenses.

I know the Tiruppāvai. Every Saturday, Sudarshana and my brother, the priest, sing Tiruppāvai and other verses from the Divyaprabandham. I wear any kind of dress for ordinary situations and special dress for special religious ones. The family is vegetarian. I eat meat. Because I am now trading in gems and often go into tribal areas, I want to accept tribal hospitality and eat with them. I am following my own life, which is a gift from God. God is in the heart. I go to all temples, mosques and churches.

If you help people, they think God does it. Whether Brahmin or non-Brahmin, everyone will die. People get different opportunities from God. Poor people have bad karma; rich people have good karma. Sādhus]] go away [[P116]] from the family and live in the street. They get respect. Gypsies live with their families. They all have the same quality of life. Life is nothing—our bodies will disappear. At death, our souls will go to Paramapadam (the supreme heaven). One needs to get rid of karma. Brahmins and non-Brahmins—all are going to die. Although Siddhas (holy men) used to live for 500 years, they also now die. Everything comes from God, although we can’t see him. Pure people have no jealousy or anger; they are helpful and selfless. Like Sai Baba. Why are the āḻvārs famous? Because they had no egos, no buildings. They expected nothing. Both rich and poor are subject to karma. I want to help; however, I also want to live.

Now I am earning just enough to live. Still, I have some rupees or clothes to give others. I now take care of my four kids. Ultimately their lives are in their own hands. I go about with ordinary people, the poor and others. True love is when you don’t expect anything. Rich people are selfish. I love the poor. When you get more things, you have more problems. The poor have fresh faces and can laugh. We follow Rāmānuja as our guru. If there was no Rāmānuja, there would be no Śrīvaiṣṇavism. I stay away from politics. And I don’t like caste. God is one. In my own family, castes are mixing.

On my final visit to the ashram, Padu told me that a huge highway would be built just behind the ashram. Everyone worried that its beauty and tranquility would soon be gone.

The Music Shrine’s Protector

I visited a Naidu Śrīvaiṣṇava family in Triplicane. T. Vijaya Raghavan, who works for Allied Publishers, a company that imports medical books for libraries, was my host and introduced me to his family. His wife is a housewife, and his sister works in an eye hospital as a general nurse. In the past, she was a dancer and performed in Kanchipuram and various sabhās. Also, I met Vijaya [[P117]] began to narrate the family’s history. ‘My family has been Śrīvaiṣṇava for several generations,’ he said. ‘The whole family has been initiated. We are disciples of Ramanuja Acarya of Sriperumbudur.’ He then told me about his family’s musical tradition.

My great grandfather, Munuswami Naidu (1880–1950), played the clarinet and the music of Tyāgarāja (1767–1847). Tyāgarāja was a Telugu Brahmin born in Tanjavur; he composed classical (Carnatic) music, especially devotional songs praising Lord Rāma. Kuppuswami Naidu, my grandfather, studied in Ambur in Vellore district with a famous vidvāṉ,39 a performer named Sri Chinnaswami. The clarinet is not caste-based. It is a Western instrument used for Carnatic music. Often, Naidus play it.40

By contrast, the nāgasvaram, a traditional Tamil reed instrument, is played only by one caste and is therefore considered a hereditary instrument. My grandfather learned mṛdaṅgam, too, from Sri Umayalpuram K. Sivaraman,41 another famous performer in Tamil Nadu. Afterwards, my grandfather had his own ‘band’, a small troupe of about ten relatives who gave concerts in the pillared hall at the Parthasarathy temple and played in processions during Brahmotsavam and other festivals. This was done as service to God, not for pay. There was no tradition of concert performances at that time.

My father, Sri Keshavalu, learned the clarinet, and my younger brother, C. K. Vasudevan, learned it from him. He has played in bands for dance and drama performances around Tamil Nadu and Singapore. He also does naṭṭuvaṅgam (a kind of song accompanied by tapping the rhythm on wood or with cymbals) and earns his living now by accompanying Alarmel Valli, a famous Indian dancer, in performances in Europe, America and around the world.42 My younger brother’s two sons play instruments as well. My sons and I play no instrument. I am more interested in devotion and service and hearing lectures about Śrīvaiṣṇavism. [[P118]]

When I asked whether Bhāgavatas face discrimination in the music world, he replied ‘I don’t think so.’ He added: ‘Even though Alarmel Valli is a non-Brahmin, she is so famous that her caste is not a problem. If a vidvāṉ is really good, there is no problem getting on stage.’ Vijaya gave a few more examples of famous non-Brahmin performers, such as A. K. C. Natarajan, who played the clarinet.43

Evidence of the family’s musical heritage is found in the music shrine next door. It is built very much like a temple, and so it is called a bhajana kōyiḻ. Vijaya performs the pūjā there every morning and during festivals. We went to see it. Vijaya proudly said that the shrine ‘was built about 120 years ago and then was passed to my grandfather Kuppuswami and down through my family. It contains many Tanjavur paintings.’

We circumambulated a very narrow walkway, with walls on either side, all painted with red and white stripes. Outside, along the back walkway, were a storage room and a kitchen. Standing in front of the sanctum, I saw a painting of Lord Nārāyaṇa. There were also small paintings of Rāmānuja and Maṇavāḷa Māmuni on either side of the central image. Just outside the sanctum, in the hall for bhajanas, were many more Tanjavur paintings, including ones of Āṇṭāḷ and Tyāgarāja. I saw a palanquin that devotees use to take images around the streets during festival processions. A Telugu inscription on the wall says that this temple is their family’s property, that daily bhajanas must be done, and that devotees must be fed during the day after Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī. The inscription had no date. Vijaya thought that it might belong to the time when the temple was built. Vijaya then explained more about this place.

Bhajanas continue to be held and many festivals are celebrated here, such as Navarātri, Mārkaḻi, Vaikuṇṭha Ekādaśī and the birthdays of Rāma and Krishna. At these times, there are morning pūjās and evening bhajanas featuring the music of Tyāgarāja and Annamācārya.44 And there are concerts with live classical music. This place is not used, however, for religious talks or sabhā meetings. [[P119]]

There is a special upstairs room next door where guests can relax between morning and evening events.

After touring the bhajana kōyiḻ, we returned to Vijaya’s home. He showed me their house shrine. Besides the sacred śalagrāma stones, there are small images. Each is 300–400 years old. There is one of the dancing Krishna, another of Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣmaṇa together, one of Lord Śrīnivāsa Perumāḷ and his consort, as well as ones of Nammāḻvār, Rāmānuja and Maṇavāla Māmuṉi. Vijaya said that he does the daily worship, offering incense, light, cooked rice, and fruits while reciting the Tiruppāvai, Tiruppallāṇṭu, and Tirupaḷḷiyeḻucci.

Every Sunday, the family goes to a Rāmānuja maṭha near the Parthasarathy temple. There, they chant selections from the Divyaprabandham. Vijaya’s sister Hema knows 400 verses, including the Tiruppāvai and Tiruppallāṇṭu. For the rest, she reads from a book. Vijaya’s son Venkatakrishnan knows 1,500 verses out of the 4,000; he needs the help of a book for the rest. In their lane is another bhajana maṭha, where seven to eight Bhāgavatas learn bhajanas from a Brahmin, Veera Raghavan, who teaches each evening. When I asked if they belonged to any sabhā, Hema mentioned one in T. Nagar and a rāmānuja kūṭam near the Parthasarathy temple, where they sometimes go to hear lectures.

One young woman commented:

I like the Tiruppāvai, because it is for everyone. We enjoy devotion to God with bhaktas who gather for recitation. A group of devotees is key to Vaiṣṇavism. Festivals bring people together. Periyāḻvār comes with us when we say the blessing pallāṇṭu (‘May you live long’) for God.45 Even the Harijans join in festivals and pull the Lord’s chariot.

Later, K. K. A. Venkatachari remarked that Vijaya’s father, Sri Keshavalu, used to attend the Tiruppāvai recitation at his home, and sat silently with the chanting Brahmins. This man died last year. Now, Vijaya and his uncle come. They chant silently, as the Brahmins chant aloud. Venkatachari says that most Bhāgavatas are not bothered that they should not chant [[P120]] with the Brahmins in a Brahmin’s home. Nonetheless, Vijaya is certainly distressed by acts of discrimination at temples. He told me:

Sixty to seventy years ago, there was a fight in Srirangam over whether Bhāgavatas could chant in the temple. The non-Brahmins lost, and now only the Brahmin goṣṭhi (a registered group) has the right to do this. At the Parthasarathy temple, non-Brahmins were given no tīrtham and no prasādam up to 1961. T. K. Ramanuja Dasar, a strict Śrīvaiṣṇava Bhāgavata from Chidambaram, visited the Parthasarathy temple. Like other Bhāgavatas, he experienced discrimination. He took his case to the Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Board. An order was passed on 20 December 1967, which said that no discrimination should be shown while distributing the tīrtham and prasādam among Śrīvaiṣṇavas at the Parthasarathy temple. Even after the Hindu Charitable Endowments Board took over management of the temple, Brahmins still cause problems for non-Brahmins.

During my visit to his home, Vijaya complained about a current injustice. This involved a clash at the old Yathoktakārī temple in Kanchipuram, between Brahmin and non-Brahmin devotees. He wanted advice from Venkatachari on how Bhāgavatas should handle this.

At the end of the interview, I brought up the topic of political affiliation. Vijaya said that he has none. Rather, he votes for individuals.

The Maker of Village Priests

P. Balakrishnan Swaamy, also known as Necavalar or Kakkalayam, has been a non-Brahmin priest (arcaka). I wanted to meet him, because I was told that he had been teaching non-Brahmin boys the Divyaprabandham as part of a government programme to train boys, including non-Brahmins, to be priests. The classes are held in a building across the street from a gate to the Parthasarathy temple in Triplicane. [[P121]]

I arrived in the midst of a class. About twenty young men dressed in white dhotis and bare-chested, except for the sacred thread across their shoulders, were sitting at desks. When Balakrishnan called on a student, he stood and chanted a verse. The students sometimes stopped or made mistakes, Balakrishnan gently corrected them, and the class moved on.

After the class, I sat down with Balakrishnan to learn more. ‘This school, which has been in operation for about a year,’ he told me,

is run by the Parthasarathy Devasthānam. There is a similar program in Srirangam run by the Srirangam Devasthānam. Most temples are now administrated by non-Brahmins. The Tamil Nadu government initiated this scheme of training non-Brahmin priests. Open to students of all ages and castes, it gives students a 500-rupee stipend, plus food and accommodation. They come from all over Tamil Nadu. This is a three-year course. Students take classes in Veda, āgama, Divyaprabandham, and Tamil. The goal is to become priests in village temples. Of the sixty-five students that attend, 95 per cent are non-Brahmins, even though there are a few Brahmins too. There is also a Śaiva school like this one, except it is located in a different place. Vaiṣṇavas must have samāśrayaṇam before joining the school, although there is no such initiation requirement for Śaiva students.

I found the government’s insistence on initiation unusual and learned that this requirement is not demanded by the government, but rather by the Śrīvaiṣṇava organisers of this programme.

‘I am one of four teachers in the new government school,’ continues Balakrishnan.

My job is to teach the Divyaprabandham. I am the only non-Brahmin teacher. The Veda, āgama and Tamil teachers are all Brahmins. I teach three times a week. So far, the students have memorised about thirty stanzas, how to chant them and how to explain their basic meaning. Because the students are from the villages, sometimes I have to explain a verse several times to them. At first, the students were not so interested. Now they are becoming more interested. The young boys must stand up and recite in the traditional way. This is considered polite.

This school has been controversial. Some Brahmins did not want it, and so they took a case to court. The government fought back, arguing ‘no caste discrimination’. There is now a Supreme Court case in Delhi [I discuss this in Chapter 7]. Not even 1 per cent of the population is against this government plan. Just a few Brahmins. Because Śrīvaiṣṇavism is caste-inclusive, the government is not against it. There is a lot of public support plus the equality clauses in the Constitution. In March, the government will extend the classes into more parts of Tamil Nadu. [[P122]]

When I met Balakrishnan again, this time outside the school, he told me this.

I am originally from Nellur in Andhra and belong to a caste of weavers. I am now fifty, married and have two sons. One has a Bachelor’s degree in engineering and has a job as an engineer. My second son is still a student in engineering. We have had initiation from the Tirukkoyilur jīyar.

I received much of my training in prabandha sevai (service related to the Divyaprabandham) and vaidika-kārya (Vedic rituals) from my father. He was a pure Vaiṣṇava, a trustee of a non-Brahmin temple in Mylapore (a Chennai neighbourhood) called Andal Rangamannar Sannidhi. He was also the priest there. I took Sanskrit as my second language in school. I know the vocabulary, but am not very proficient in the grammar. Most of my Sanskrit knowledge I got from my father. I like the āḻvārs and prefer the Tamil Divyaprabandham to Sanskrit texts. I became a priest in the same temple where my father was the priest.

I no longer officiate as a priest. Besides teaching at the new government school in Triplicane, I do private chanting and give ‘discourses’ not for money, rather from the heart. Still, the payment given to me helps provide income for my family. In addition, I perform the last rites, annual śrāddhas (the rituals for one’s deceased ancestors) and weddings. I did them for Vijaya Raghavan’s family.46

I am the last one in my family doing this kind of work. It gives me a lot of respect. I am now doing prabandha sevai discourses in many places.

I asked Balakrishnan if Bhāgavatas should perform samāśrayaṇam for others. ‘No,’ he told me, and then explained why:

Rāmānuja established the seventy-four families for performing the initiation ritual. He selected only these Brahmin families to be ācāryas. Moreover, for the initiation ritual, one needs preliminary adhikāra, that is, scriptural expertise. Only the ācāryas have this qualification because they know Sanskrit well.

Next, I asked: ‘Can non-Brahmins join the Brahmin group or goṣṭhi chanting the Divyaprabandham in the temple?’ Balakrishnan began his answer by referring to Nammāḻvār’s passage about the Cāṇḍāḷas (Tiruvāymoḻi 3.7.9) and said, ‘Once Bhāgavatas have had initiation, they too can chant the Divyaprabandham.’ I remarked that even now, non-Brahmins are not allowed to chant near the sanctum in many temples, and he replied:

This is wrong; it is against the sampradāya. Nammāḻvār, who composed the Tiruvāymoḻi, himself is a non-Brahmin. Therefore, Brahmins can’t reject non-Brahmins. Some Brahmins are wrong, not the sampradāya. To give discourses in the temple twenty years ago was impossible. Then the government ordered that this be allowed in Parthasarathy temple. The government supports non-Brahmins.

Our conversation expanded on the relations of Brahmins and non-Brahmins. Balakrishnan commented:

Śrīvaiṣṇavism has four classes (varṇas). Each has its own dharma (occupation and duties). If a śūdra becomes a Śrīvaiṣṇava through initiation, he becomes a Bhāgavata. Once people of any varṇa are initiated, they are dependent only on God and become Rāmānuja-dāsas (servants of [[P123]] Rāmānuja). Therefore, all four classes become Rāmānuja-dāsas, including the Brahmins. [[P124]]

This is the theory, if not the practice. Only with initiation does one become connected to Rāmānuja. Once we belong to the sampradāya, we have to act according to Rāmānuja’s values and so varṇa-dharma applies only to rituals. For example, Brahmins must do certain rituals, whereas others do ‘customs’ according to the Rāmānuja sampradāya. Śāstra (Vedic scripture) is the basis for Brahmin rituals. It is sanātana-dharma, eternal, and always there. One is a Brahmin only if one knows the Vedas and does Vedic rituals. If a Brahmin does other work, he is not a Brahmin. Character is important too. Viśvamitra was born as a Kṣatriya and became a sage (ṛṣi) by character. Birth is not important; occupation and character are.

If a non-Brahmin studies the Vedas and Vedic ritual, he is a Brahmin, even though Brahmins do not accept this idea. Priesthood is becoming a job with a commercial orientation: varṇa is eternal, even though defined by occupation and character, not birth. Before, non-Brahmins were subservient. Now that is changing. Why? Because Bhāgavatas are learning everything. Brahmins are becoming office workers and secular teachers; some Brahmins are becoming poor. Therefore, there is equalisation going on. After 500 years, there will be no caste. Or maybe this will take only twenty years. There have been many changes in society. Before, non-Brahmins were prohibited from entering a 1-kilometre area around Brahmin establishments. That no longer occurs.

When I asked Balakrishnan about his political affiliation, he said:

I don’t belong to a political party. Last time, I voted DMK. Even though Karunanidhi is an atheist, which is bad, he believes in no caste. Therefore, I like this party’s position. Before that, I had voted twenty years for Congress, because I like Gandhi’s philosophy. [[P125]]

The next year, when I visited Triplicane, I enquired about the government school and was told that it was no longer running after a year. No one knew why, although there were speculations. According to one person, the government was just politically motivated and not interested in carrying through its plan. Of course, there was also a case before the Supreme Court; authorities might have decided to await the outcome. I will return to this topic of access to the priesthood in Chapter 7.

The Lawyer and his Service

V. Raza Narayanan started the interview by telling me that he belongs to the Nayaka (that is, the Vanniyar) caste.

Nayakas were once warriors and therefore are technically Kṣatriyas who descended from Pallava and Cōḻa rulers. Many generations ago, my family had a palace near Chidambaram. I was born into a Vaiṣṇava family. The word ‘Hindu’ for identification is new. Before, people said that they belonged to a Vaiṣṇava family or a Śaiva one. On the front door of my house is a huge nāmam. My grandfather, Mr Sadar Adikesavalu Nayakar, who wore a big nāmam, lived in Madurantakam, where Rāmānuja long ago had received samāśrayaṇam. My grandparents dressed as traditional Śrīvaiṣṇavas. My grandfather wore a dhoti and had a hair tuft like the Brahmins. Unlike the Brahmins, he wore a turban. My grandfather had initiation at Sriperumbudur.

During the lifetime of Gandhi, my family supported the Congress party. In 1939, my grandfather gave the welcome address to Gandhi when he came to Chennai. My father, a lawyer, was a great orator in both Tamil and English. A freedom fighter and Member of the Legislative Assembly (MLA), he attended the first Indian parliamentary constituent assembly, which was set up in 1946, and was a member of the first parliament after independence in 1947. He wrote about Vaiṣṇavism. After his death, I collected his essays into three books and published them. And I endowed a lecture series in his honour. My mother also studied law. She did not finish, however, because she [[P126]] had me. I, too, am a lawyer. My daughter, however, wants to be an engineer.

Our conversation turned to religion. Raza described his daily religious activities and his views on some Śrīvaiṣṇava traditions.

In the morning, I chant the Tiruppaḷḷāṇṭu, Tirupaḷḷeḻucci, Tiruppāvai, Kaṇṇinuṇciṟuttāmpu and twelve songs about Lord Parthasarathy. I do this while going about my work, because I must do my karma and prepare for court. Then, I offer tulasi at my home shrine. I don’t sit to chant, because that is just to impress others. My life is very hectic. Yesterday alone, I had to appear for three cases in three courts in three parts of the city. My duty is to protect my clients, and so work must take precedence over religion. Still, I manage to read religious books in bed at night and listen to religious cassettes.

I oppose samāśrayaṇam, because it is not practical. It makes me leave my own traditions and lifestyle, making me a slave to the Brahmins. Take a Brahmin as an ācārya, and you become a slave to him. In Gītā 18:66, Lord Krishna says, ‘leave all dharmas and come to me’ [in other words, reject all duties of caste and stage of life, according to some interpretations; there are others]. Āṇṭāḷ’s Tiruppāvai 5 says, ‘We have come to you pure.’ This doesn’t mean having a sacred bath, rather, having a pure heart. One should bring pure flowers and worship only with love.

People should stand on their own two legs. ‘Give them fishing rods, not fish.’ It is important to teach skills. For development, there should be no discrimination. Today, according to the government, anyone can be a priest. Even now, many non-Brahmins are priests. Non-Brahmins are better chanters and orators than Brahmins. Brahmins just want money, although they claim that they have better qualifications, including knowledge of the Veda, ability to preach, expertise to perform ritual yāgams, competent teaching and contacts necessary for donations. Show me one Brahmin who has all of these qualifications.

Brahmins are just Brahmins by skin colour. In fact, the one whom they think is their father might not be their real father, for only a mother knows for sure. Rather, someone who knows Brahman, the supreme reality, is a Brahmin. If you call yourself a doctor, you must know medicine. And so, if you call yourself a Brahmin, you must know the Veda. Wearing a dhoti and nāmam is not enough. Do the Brahmins at Parthasarathy temple know the āḻvārs’ names and titles of their works?47 [[P127]] No. By contrast, I know all the āḻvārs’ verses by heart.

We must conduct religious camps for Vaiṣṇavas. There is not enough infrastructure. There should be services for children, such as evening schools on each street. Children should memorise the short āḻvār works and participate in prayer meetings. Forget the Sanskrit Vedas. The New Testament (the Tamil hymns of the āḻvārs) has come, so let the Old Testament (the Veda) go. It is useless, when the Tamil Veda is here.

One should go to Tirupati for devotion, not for bargaining with God, as in ‘If I give you X, you give me Y.’ When I go to any temple, I go just to chant the āḻvār songs about that temple. I don’t go to beg. God has given me everything. I need nothing more. We should go to our God to thank him, not to beg. If you ask him for something, this means that you don’t believe he will give you that. Just thanking God is true devotion. Tirupati is lots of ‘business’.

I want Vaiṣṇavism to be everywhere, because I feel that everyone is a Vaiṣṇava. Viṣṇu is the pervader. He is in everyone. Therefore, I say, ‘don’t discriminate. When he is in even the non-living, how can you discriminate among living things?’ A Vaiṣṇava is for all and in all. Therefore, a Vaiṣṇava should go to all. This is a simple religion with a high philosophy, which everyone can practice. If you feel part of God, there can be no enmity with anyone. There should be no discrimination against anyone. Therefore, this is the only religion that can survive. It is for the people of the world. Even Christians and Muslims are really Vaiṣṇavas, although they might not know this. God is in churches and mosques, too.

Religion has nothing to do with politics. The Bharatiya Janata Party is just using religion. Politicians are only [[P128]] interested in religion to gain the votes of the devotees. Because of vote-bank politics, they have little interest at heart in the people. For us, God and service to him are more important than politics.

It was obvious that Raza’s religious life was an expression of kaiṅkarya. He wanted to communicate his knowledge to others.

I lecture on the Tiruppāvai during the thirty days of Mārkaḻi. Whenever I am invited to lecture, I do. I refuse to accept money for this. It is my donation to the Vaiṣṇava Mahasangam. Nothing can be better than the āḻvārs. They wanted to be near the people in a language that the people can understand. They created a Veda in Tamil.

One evening, I went to the Sri Manavala Mamunigal temple (at Alwarpet, a neighbourhood of Chennai), an important non-Brahmin Chennai temple, where Raza was teaching a group of Bhāgavatas how to recite and understand the āḻvār hymns. He had told me that this is a temple without discrimination: no class, no gender, and no dress distinctions. His commitment to this temple is strong. Several years later, in October 2015, I noticed on the Internet that he was lecturing there on the first three āḻvārs. Raza was also active in a major non-Brahmin organisation.

I have been the general secretary of the Vaiṣṇava Mahasangam, which is sponsored by the Narayana Dhanam Trust. It began in 1925, when five non-Brahmins were attending a Vaiṣṇava conference in Triplicane. They were asked to leave and told that they had no right to hear these things and no right to sit with the Brahmins. Because Raghava Ramanuja Swami wanted to have a common platform so that all Bhāgavatas could have access to knowledge and discussion, he started the Vaiṣṇava Mahasangam. A women’s section began in 1930; it argued for the rights of women to maintenance and property. Every month, we meet to propagate Vaiṣṇavism. [[P129]]

All the āḻvārs’ birthdays are celebrated. Bhāgavatas sit together and read that āḻvār’s hymns. There is no discrimination. All are welcome. Women come as well as men. There are no dress rules, no caste restrictions. Even Muslims and Christians have attended for ‘knowledge’. Those who come are given a copy of the Divyaprabandham and pictures of the āḻvārs, first one and eventually all twelve. After that, they receive statues of them. The pūjā is done by the devotees, the Rāmānuja Dāsas. It includes chanting from the Divyaprabandham and ārātti. Āratti merely takes the evil spirits away. For Brahmins, ārātti is a custom only to collect money.

I learned that this annual four-day conference of the Vaisnava Mahasangam attracts non-Brahmins (mainly Vanniyars) from all over Tamil Nadu. It takes place during December, in an elegant old colonial building on Govindappa-naicken Street (in the Georgetown area in Chennai), which had once belonged to Vuppattur Alvarchetty (a Vaiśya businessman and philanthropist). He bequeathed it to Vaiṣṇavas for use as a rāmānuja kūṭam. Members rent it out for Vaiṣṇava marriages, meetings, and other events.

The main hall is decorated with a collection of old Tanjavur glass paintings that illustrate the Rāmāyaṇa. It also has a large picture of P. B. Annangaracariyar (1874–1936) of Kanchipuram. According to K. K. A. Venkatachari, he was a Brahmin Teṉkaḷai Śrīvaiṣṇava, learned scholar, a marvellous orator with a wonderful memory and voice, famous author,48 and editor of five magazines. He regularly gave discourses here, but would stop them during the conference, which he supported strongly.

The day that I visited was the seventy-fifth anniversary of the women’s organisation, and one of the two days of the annual event especially for women. (Another day was for youth.) Because this was the month of Mārkaḻi, there were lectures on Āṇṭāḷ. One woman told me that Āṇṭāḷ is an important model of empowerment, because she had gathered together girls for their ritual bath, and to serve the Lord. ‘If women act collectively in the name of the Lord,’ she said, ‘they will be empowered.’ [[P130]]

That day, the organisation was also honouring thirty-eight authors who had written books on Vaiṣṇavism, mainly in Tamil. And it was honouring those over seventy who had dedicated themselves to service in temple kitchens, gardens, and rāmānuja kūṭams; they are Śrīvaiṣṇava selvars (‘royal’ people in the service of God). In addition, there were daily concerts and speeches by Bhāgavatas from all over south India. And there was a communal evening meal.

Raza had a very broad concept of service, which went far beyond his religious activities as kaiṅkarya:

I am also secretary of the Bharat Sewak Samaj. It is a pan-Indian organization, founded by former Prime Minister Nehru. It has chapters all over India. It is intended for social workers and is secular. There is a three-month training programme in social work for college students, which leads to a certificate. Students go to orphanages, schools for the blind, and so forth to see the real world. They are taught about the importance of working actively to benefit society.

The Bharat Sewak Samaj has a students’ wing, a youth wing and leadership camps. Funding comes from patrons and friends, not the government, which would make it political and therefore subservient to government bureaucracy. Every month, the organisation celebrates a national leader’s birthday. In this way, it provides role models for young people. I am a member of the Humour Club as well. It is open to everyone and teaches people how to criticise others with laughter and politeness. Some go on to have careers in films. As well, I encourage drama and music sabhās and studies of contemporary society. [[P131]]

Service was certainly a common orientation for my informants, although the specific activities varied from one person to another.

The Lord’s Proselytiser

T. Bhasya Ramanuja Dasar belongs to the Beri Cettiyar,49 a caste of small businessmen. He grew up in Tirumani, near Chengalpattu. He has lived in Chennai for many years. Varataa

Ethiraja Jeeyar of Sriperumbudur initiated him. When I visited Bhasya, he was in the Sri Nampillai Sabhai, in Chintadripet near Mount Road, the oldest part of Chennai.

The sabhā, founded in 1889 by some Bhāgavatas, is named after the thirteenth-century figure Nampiḷḷai, who had given many learned yet popular discourses on the Tiruvāymoḻi, which were recounted by Vaṭakku Tiruvīti Piḷḷai in his commentary known as the Īṭu. This sabhā used to meet every Saturday in the front yard of the house of a Śrīvaiṣṇava who gave talks on religion.

In 1980, Krishnaswami Reddiyar, a justice in the Madras High Court, was elected president of the sabhā and, with his new committee, collected money through donations and loans to construct a new building, which opened in 1990. This building has three shops in it; the income from the rent in addition to donations from devotees make possible the daily pūjā to the pictures of God Rāma and others kept on the premises. Ten or fifteen years ago, I took over the maintenance of the sabhā’s building and manage it.

Every week, a Bhāgavata lectures on Śrīvaiṣṇava thought and someone else teaches the Divyaprabandham in a traditional way: a teacher reads one line from a stanza, and the student repeats it three times. At monthly meetings, members talk about spreading Vaiṣṇavism. Every month, the birth stars, that is, the birthdays, of the āḻvārs and ācāryas born in that month are celebrated by singing all 4,000 verses.

During the festival that celebrates Rāmānuja’s birthday (brahmotsavam), the sabhā conducts a camp at Sriperumbudur to teach Bhāgavatas the meanings of Amutaṉār’s Rāmānuja Nuṟṟantāti in praise of Rāmānuja.

During November and December (the lunar month of Kārttikai), the sabhā conducts its annual festival of Nampiḷḷai for ten days, and Bhāgavatas recite the 4,000 hymns. It gives prizes to students who came first in Tamil in the state and the district. Also, it gives free school uniforms and books for five students in the immediate area, provides medical help for others, serves a free lunch [[P132]] for 100 people at a local temple, and provides buttermilk to devotees who pull the temple chariot on festival days at the Sriperumbudur and Triplicane temples.

Bhāgavata leaders have devoted themselves to educating Bhāgavatas about their religious tradition by publishing cheap Tamil pamphlets and books. I myself have brought out many such publications, some of them for free distribution.

Bhasya showed me some of these magazines and booklets, which contain articles by him and others. Many contained basic information and edifying stories about the tradition. One issue of the magazine Eṇṇāyiramum Irāmānucaruman, for example, includes the story of Rāmānuja’s preference for a poor woman—she is willing to prostitute herself to get money to buy food for him and his entourage—instead of his richer and more egotistical disciples.

Another publication provides a collection of verses from the Divyaprabandham, which are often recited together.50 The booklet Śrī Kūrattāḻvāṉ provides a short biography of Kūrattāḻvāṉ (Kūreśa), who was Rāmānuja’s scribe. An issue of Tirumāmakaḷ comments at length on a stanza of Amutaṉar’s Rāmānuja Nuṟṟantāti, discusses the importance of two months, Kārttikai and Mārkaḻi, for Vaiṣṇavas, and analyses one stanza from the Tiruppāvai.

These publications include discussions about problems that Śrīvaiṣṇavism is facing and what to do about them. Śrīvaiṇavar: Yesterday, Today, and Tomorrow, for instance, discusses the future of Vaiṣṇavism and makes the following suggestions: Bhāgavatas should avoid preachers who cannot describe religious matters properly for the common people, and they should criticise those who commercialise Vaiṣṇavism, or claim to be collecting money for local temples, while actually stealing it. In addition, they should criticise those who claim that they are protecting Vaiṣṇavism, despite the fact that they can recite only a few Sanskrit stanzas. [[P133]]

An article by Bhasya in the Tirumāmakaḷ51 says that ācāryas and jīyars, in earlier times, discussed the problems of Śrīvaiṣṇavas. The ācārya lineages have virtually disappeared today. The jīyars who head the maṭhas come with their disciples in six or seven cars to attend functions with ‘pomp and show’, and then return to their comfortable maṭhas, which are ‘like heaven’. Some of these maṭhas are accountable to no one, including the government (even though the government partially regulates the big ones). Maṭhas now control the lands that had been donated to Śrīvaiṣṇava temples; the jīyars spend the income for display, though, and not for the benefit of religion. Many maṭhas do not even have complete records of their own properties and therefore lose an enormous amount of money. Moreover, temples now use 200 litres of milk every month for bathing Rāmānuja’s image during the celebration of the birth-star (birthday) of Rāmānuja. The jīyars, however, refrain from criticising this extravagance.

And here is another problem. Temples hide their wealth, so communities do not know how much there is. Recently, in the birthplace of Kūreśa, the priest’s son stole the temple’s jewels and sold them. Only after a month did the villagers hear about this. Magazines reported very different amounts of what was stolen. With a proper board in control, of course, the true value would have been clear to everyone. In short, maṭhas that do not follow the tradition strictly are beneficial to neither devotees nor the public.

Consider the case of the son of a Brahmin priest who had married a Muslim woman. He wanted to be a priest. Because the temple rejected him, he went to court and won the case. No Śrīvaiṣṇava maṭha objected. Even though this case occurred outside India, says the author, it could be used as a precedent in Tamil Nadu (presumably for Muslim control of Hindu temples or government control of religion). The article concludes that these irresponsible jīyars should be relegated to the ‘garden’, since they cannot return to family life and should not participate in temple life.

In this publication, Bhasya describes other problems that the jīyars ignore. One is connecting Rāmānuja with astrology. About ten years ago, an astrologer announced that anyone [[P134]] affected in his or her horoscope by Rahu (a shadowy planet) should perform the bathing ritual with milk to an image of Rāmānuja. Although there was no precedent for this, no jīyar objected. Moreover, someone published an astrological book and claimed that it was a genuine work of Rāmānuja. No jīyar objected or bothered to find out the truth. Bhasya tells us that he himself objected in his journal and made the publisher apologise. Then there was the matter of Agnihotra Ramanuja Tattacarya, who wrote in a general magazine about Lord Raṅganātha’s sex life with goddess Lakṣmī. Many Śrīvaiṣṇavas found this article disturbing, Bhasya remarked, because it created the ‘wrong impression’. Once again, comments Bhasya, the jīyars ignored a significant problem.

These discussions of problems in the magazines are interspersed with advice on how to handle them. One is to engage the younger generation in religious affairs. Young people should take responsibility for giving lectures on Vaiṣṇava subjects in a language that everyone can understand. More generally, every village and thickly-populated non-Brahmin district in a city should have a bhajana maṭha or bhajana kōyiḻ, where people can gather once a week, or even more often, to sing the Divyaprabandham or Rāma or Krishna bhajanas to the beat of cymbals. More than 10 of these groups meet in Chennai. Moreover, Śrīvaiṣṇavas should use these occasions to promote the sampradāya and invite other good Śrīvaiṣṇava scholars to deliver special lectures.

As for the maṭhas, Bhasya points out that a board of honest and impartial Vaiṣṇavas should control them and their funds should be available to all Vaiṣṇava temples. On a more political note, Bhasya tells readers to give their votes only to politicians who support their local temples.52

These discussions of problems in the magazines provide examples of how the printing press has helped democratise knowledge. It has encouraged Bhāgavatas to educate other Bhāgavatas on their own terms and in their own sabhā communities.

Bhasya told me about the Srivainavar Peravai, an organisation that he began in 1998 in his village (Tirumani), [[P135]] and has continued to lead from its subsequent base in Chennai. It now has 1,500 chapters all over Tamil Nadu. Its purpose is to discuss non-Brahmin social problems and to recruit non-Brahmins to Śrīvaiṣṇavism. The chapters, he explained, are established in the following way.

Members of the Srivainavar Peravai might alert a village that members, usually about twelve, will arrive to tell them about Śrīvaiṣṇavism and its sabhā organisations. Or a village might request their visit. The Srivainavar Peravai group travels in a big van, covering fifteen to twenty villages in a day. In the villages, they chant verses from the Divyaprabandham, accompanied by drums and harmoniums.53 They march through the streets carrying pictures of Rāmānuja and Āṇṭāḷ, eventually collecting 100 or 200 people. In the city of Cuddalore, as many as 2,000 joined the procession.

In addition to this nagara-saṅkīrtana (singing saṅkīrtanas in the city), they do the following: lecture, distribute free copies of the Tiruppāvai or the Divyaprabandham, and hold bhajana competitions for people who come from several villages and compete for prizes such as a harmonium. They also give plates that have been embossed with pictures of Rāmānuja to Śrīvaiṣṇavas who did something special, such as travelling 11,000 kilometres to many of the divyadeśas.

Today, Bhāgavatas’ chanting of verses in the sabhās and during visits to villages draws on both the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition and that of pan-Indian bhajanas.

Bhasya described how the ritual called bhajana paddhati began during the fifteenth century in Tirupati and took definitive shape during the eighteenth in Tanjavur. This is a sequence of songs by poet-composers. They describe people who seek communion with God.54 Simon Leopold thinks that the ritualised march of the Śrīvaiṣṇava nagara-saṅkīrtana, organised by the urban Bhāgavatas, might have originated in a Smārta procession called uñcchavṛtti. Devotees acted out the life of Tyāgarāja (1767–1847) in public processions through villages in the area of Tanjavur. But, I think, one need not turn to a Smārta [[P136]] procession for origin; processions have been ubiquitous in Tamil Nadu and elsewhere.

In ‘Aims and Objectives of the Śrīvaiṇavar Peravai’, Bhasya comments:

you can see real Vaiṣṇavism only in villages. This is why we travel to so many of them, now over 6,000, to give lectures. The Peravai also visits schools. In Cuddalore, for instance, we gave Tiruppāvais to 1,200 students and sent circulars to announce that a teacher would be available to prepare the kids for competitions. Later, the students took tests. They had to recite all twenty-nine stanzas and interpret some of them.55

A proselytising perspective, I would add, lies at the core of Śrīvaiṣṇavism. Bhasya mentions a hagiographic story about Rāmānuja. When he goes to worship, he holds the hands of his two disciples, Mutaliyāṇṭāṉ and Piḷḷāṉ. One time, Mutaliyāṇṭāṉ says, ‘In all deśas, and in whatever time, may the great valor of Rāmānuja grow and grow without any obstruction.’ Then Piḷḷāṉ composes a verse, which says that the divine commandments of Rāmānuja should grow every year, reach all the directions and shine. Another pious man holds Rāmānuja’s hand and, alluding to him, says: ‘May the concept of Śrīman, the wealth of Srirangam, increase’.56

In fact, says the scholar Kandadai Seshadri (whose book was published in 1998), until recently the ācāryas would visit villages, where they were treated with acts of hospitality. They not only gave religious talks and answered questions about the religion, but they also tried to resolve disputes so that villagers did not have to resort to the feudal courts, and would accept the decision because of its religious sanctity. The ācāryas also intervened on behalf of the Vaiṣṇava outcastes ‘to lessen their burden’.57 According to K. K. A. Venkatachari, although Śrīvaiṣṇava ācāryas themselves went to the villages by bullock cart to spread Śrīvaiṣṇavism, they stopped doing so in the twentieth century because land-redistribution programmes and secular jobs took too much of their resources and time.58 [[P137]]

I sensed that there was considerable competition among religious sects. One Bhāgavata told me that for the past sixty years, many non-Brahmins have been attracted to the Ayyapa tradition. Another Bhāgavata said that the Ayyapa religion offered more worldly benefits (he did not say what these were) than worshipping Nārāyaṇa did. As a good Śrīvaiṣṇava, however, he would not go to another deity. Some Sai Baba disciples once asked him to give a lecture to them. He refused to wear their vibhūti, however, and thought only of God Nārāyaṇa as they were chanting their mantras.

Vaiṣṇavas compete with Śaivas as well. Another Bhāgavata told me that Śaivism might attract more Bhāgavatas than Vaiṣṇavism because their temples are less strict than Śrīvaiṣṇava ones. If one wants to go to the Śrīvaiṣṇava temple after work and is dressed in Western clothes, one may not enter the sanctum. This is not the case in Śaiva temples. Bhasya noted that Śaivas invite Śrīvaiṣṇavas to recite the Tiruvempāvai and Tiruppāvai, and both communities now join together to recite these popular poems during the month of Mārkaḻi—but Śaivas do not allow Śrīvaiṣṇavas to distribute Tiruppāvais.

When I asked Bhasya if these non-Brahmin organisations are connected with the national BJP or the RSS, he replied, ‘These Śrīvaiṣṇava organisations are purely religious.’ [[P138]]

The Business Family’s Reflections

I am in a taxi going along Poonamalle High Road in Chennai, looking for Pettukola Towers. And there it is. A grand high-rise, a sign of Chennai’s economic boom. It is obviously owned by the family that I am about to interview, the Pettukolas, because I had already learned that they are a very prosperous Vaiśya (non-Brahmin, twice-born) family with businesses in pharmaceuticals, hospital equipment, and many other things. India is known as the pharmacy of the world, and Chennai is one of its hubs. After introductions, my conversation about religion with several family members—Mr Pettukola senior (Pettukola Sriramulu Bhashyam), his wife (Pettukola Kalavathi Bhashyam), and their son Gnaneshwar Pettukola—begins.

Mr Pettukola senior: ‘We are a Telugu Vaiśya (Arya Vysya) family, originally from Andhra.59 My parents were Śrīvaiṣṇavas and had samāśrayaṇam. My mother was a great devotee. I wear the sacred thread. My son doesn’t, saying that it is too hard to keep clean.’

Mrs. Pettukola: ‘My father was a Śrīvaiṣṇava scholar, like a Brahmin, and studied the Brahmasūtras. Someone taught him Vedas in his small town. People used to prostrate themselves to him. My father was a lawyer and businessman.’

Mr Pettukola senior: ‘Brahmins had a monopoly. Periyar led the non-Brahmins. Now non-Brahmins want to rule rather than the higher castes. That is good. The country is so changed. What is this distinction between Brahmins and non-Brahmins? The Brahmins’ memory is well-developed. Because of millennia of discipline, they have become so clever. Despite that, today their children go to parties just like śūdras. People should learn the general principles of Śrīvaiṣṇavism, such as total surrender to God, and eliminate the Vedas and the priests who do Āgamic rituals.’

Gnaneshwar Pettukola: ‘Every Indian now says this is my country. This was a very distinctive development, because it was done peacefully. I am attracted to Gandhi.’ Changing the topic, he recounted an adage: ‘The servant asks, “why is the master … doing pūjās?” The servant takes jaggery (a form of cane sugar) in his hand and calls [God] Nārāyaṇa … saying “come”. Then Nārāyaṇa comes. Nārāyaṇa eats the jaggery. The master says, “I’ve been doing pūjā, but he [God] never came.” Then he prostrates himself before the servant and says, “you are a real Nārāyaṇa devotee.”’

Mr Pettukola senior: ‘I used to do kuṇḍalinī yoga and sit for three hours. I would ask Gnaneswar, when he was a small boy, to sit for an hour. Sādhana is to purify you for God’s grace. Still, there are easier methods. Now, I do [[P139]] mental pūjā, simple yogic postures and *japa-*chanting of the names of many deities. I am a Vaiśya and have learned to get around in this world. Similarly, we need the help of the nine navagrahas (planets), the devatās (minor deities) and finally Lord Nārāyaṇa. I worship Nārāyaṇa-Viṣṇu because Vēṅkateśvara is our family deity. It is important to get the real principles. Bhakti is an emotional approach. I don’t accept the literalism of Vaikuṇṭha, supreme heaven, or the Ocean of Milk, the cosmic ocean. They are symbols. We need name and form only for concentration.’

Gnaneshwar Pettukola: ‘If I call it a mere symbol, then I won’t be devout.’

Mr Pettukola senior: ‘The common person requires something concrete. Ramakrishna says, “Kill the goddess, to go to the next stage.” I exercise the mind. I believe in the arcā (the image) because it is there in me. It is a means, like it was for the Buddha, not an end. All is māyā, illusion. It is my limitation. Ultimately there is only the perception of truth within. The ego creates differences; the higher identity is beyond difference. We reach Nārāyaṇa or Śiva or whatever god. I am secular yet deeply religious. One should meditate with a pure mind and get the nectar of immortality.’

Gnaneshwar Pettukola: ‘We are all created through the grace of the Lord. God acts through man. Nothing is good or bad. I am against actions to fulfill desires (kāmya-karma).’

Mr Pettukola senior: ‘Karma, through parents, is genetically structured. Do you have to submit to it? Man can change and purify himself. When one believes in God, karma is mitigated. Grace comes in; bad things go away. Truth is changeless, eternal. Science is the essence of dharma (the order of the universe); it is the essence of the Vedas. This is all true science. Our stories are an allegory of science. The story of churning the milk ocean, for instance, is about evolution; everything started from the ocean. Permanent truths are the expression of the essence of the Vedas. When my wife was pregnant, she listened to auspicious sounds—works such as the Viṣṇu-sahasranāma [[P140]] and the Suprabhātam. Vedic knowledge is subtle science today.’

I sometimes ran into Gnaneshwar at a coffee shop and occasionally met him and his wife Ramā—who runs IND RASAYAN (a large private company) with her husband— for dinner. Our conversations focussed on Śrīvaiṣṇavism, although my summary here draws mainly from Gnaneshwar’s three essays, which are now on his blog.60 They express his own existential involvement with Śrīvaiṣṇavism. This is focussed on the concept of prapatti or surrender, and how this should be understood as a universal and not a parochial concept.

Gnaneshwar argues his case by drawing on proof texts from the āḻvārs and ācāryas. First, there are millions of devotees who visit Viṣṇu temples across the world, even though they do not categorise themselves formally as Vaiṣṇavas. Second, in an egalitarian society, who can deny these devotees their surrender to God? Third, Śrīvaiṣṇavism promises easy accessibility to God through surrender, so there can be no restrictions on access, such as a person’s village, family, and caste. Fourth, Śrīvaiṣṇavism says that devotees may worship God in whatever form they want. Fifth, the nāyaṉmārs, who lived at the same time as the āḻvārs, did prapatti to Lord Śiva. So, all people surrender to the one God who is known by different names. Sixth, because Śrīvaiṣṇava theology has been the subject of research by scholars from religions across the world, it is obvious that its teachings hold great appeal to non-Vaiṣṇavas as well. Seventh, Śrīvaiṣṇavism teaches that God dwells within every individual as the antaryāmi. A person who is aware of this will strive to uplift society at large, without considering caste or religion. [[P141]]

Because of this universalistic aspect of Śrīvaiṣṇavism, Gnaneshwar thinks that it should be popularised for the benefit of all mankind.

I wish to state that if I, as a Vaishnavite, live in an illusory denial that these great truths are to be adhered to only by those born into my religion or sect, it would be like a Gujarati born into the community to which Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi belonged, declaring that the Mahatma’s satyāgraha cannot be performed by anyone other than members of his own community.61

Gnaneshwar tries to reconcile the idea of Śrīvaiṣṇavism’s universalistic teachings with the reality of its division into two rival sects (the Teṉkalai and the Vaṭakalai) by locating all their similarities and showing how misinterpretations have arisen over different emphases.

Therefore, friends, we have seen that in several aspects there is an identity of intention in the minds of the Ācāryas of the Tenkalais and Vadakalais. As in the words of [Vedanta] Desika himself …. The differences therefore lie in the interpretations only … we can still stand rigid dwelling upon the many differences of opinion on intricate doctrinaire issues. If we imprison our minds thus in prejudice, we do ourselves a great disservice; for the Lord stands beckoning to us all in his limitless grace and I would rather partake in His bliss than allow my distrust and disbelief in one tradition or the other prevent me from partaking in that joy. For as Mao, the Chinese leader, once said, it does not matter whether the cat is black or white as long as it catches the mouse.62

In his essay on Nammāḻvār, Gnaneshwar begins by noting that Nammāḻvār praises Nārāyaṇa as the supreme god who is superior to all other gods. Although Gnaneshwar admits that this sounds like parochial dogma, or even bigotry, he argues that it is really a matter of unshakable faith that has completely transformed the person, as does the miraculous healing of a terminal illness or the ability to walk on fire.

Gnaneshwar often turns to modern analogies to elucidate his theological point and to appeal to young people. He makes references to shooting stars, genetic codes, scientists such as Einstein, or technology such as the Hubble telescope. With reference to faith, for instance, he points out that just as a printer will print data only when connected to the central processing unit of the computer, so the soul will find supreme bliss [[P142]] only when utterly dependent on the Lord.63 Put otherwise, one must reject the idea of independence.

Gnaneshwar’s own service, or kaiṅkarya, is to help revive the Sriperumbudur Sanskrit College64 (Ubhaya Vedanta Vardhi), which was established in 1872 by Sri Singam Chetty Ateendrooloo Chetty Garu, a Telugu-speaking man. It has had the patronage of several Chetty caste people, if I judge correctly from its list of past presidents. It has been affiliated with the University of Madras, moreover, for post-graduate studies in Sanskrit grammar and logic. Today, its mission statement is about providing Sanskrit instruction for ‘school going children who are eager to imbibe their ancient traditions’, because ‘it is a fact that Sanskrit, being an important medium towards this end, is becoming a much-demanded subject’.65 Its website announces a lecture series called Svatantra Chintana (Freedom of Thought) in the Department of Sanskrit at the University of Madras.

My next meeting with Gnaneshwar was in 2016. We discussed whether non-Brahmins should be trained as priests and what that might involve. We also discussed the many social changes occurring in south Indian society, and how challenging arranging marriages and parenting had become as young people became more independent.

The Merchant and his Medium

In Chennai, I visited P. V. Prasad and his 86-year-old uncle in an elegant old house. Prasad first briefed me on the family’s history.

We belong to the Balija (Naidu) Puligar community. Balijas were chieftains in the early sixteenth century under the Vijayanagara ruler Krishnadevaraya. Our mother tongue is Telugu. Our family had land in a village called Arani in north Arcot district. We were camphor and diamond merchants. About 100 years ago, the family came to Chennai. [[P143]]

The family still has a temple in Walajahpet, north Arcot (now Tiruvallur district). It was built about three or four generations back, because at that time no member of our family had children and hoped that if they built a temple, God would grace them with progeny. It is called Śrīnivāsa Perumāḷ temple. We hire a Brahmin priest, a Pañcarātrin, to do the worship with a mantra ending with namaḥ. This is the style of worship in the Telugu communities of this area. It is difficult to get a priest today. We are very lucky, because this priest has worked for us for thirty-five years, and his son might continue because he has had no modern education.

We have not had samāśrayaṇam, mainly because we don’t want to give up eating meat. Even many Brahmins now are not vegetarian. They are no longer strict in their values and lifestyle and have become Westernised. The nāmam is worn mainly by Brahmins; we wear only a red dot.

When I asked about their daily pūjā, the old man found a little booklet in Telugu script, containing the Strī Stuti, Viṣṇusahasranāma, Śrī Sūktam, Rāma Stotra and Āditya Stotra, and read a bit from this. Then Prasad told me the following. ‘I know no āḻvār hymns; rather, I only chant the mantra oṁ namo nārāyaṇāya. Sometimes, I visit Viṣṇu, Hari Hara, Śiva and Murukaṉ temples. I go to Tirupati every year and Srirangam once every three or four years.’

Prasad’s religious life extends beyond Śrīvaiṣṇavism. This takes us to Agastiya. Hindus believe that Agastiya was one of the seven ancient Vedic sages. In south India, his followers think that he is the transmitter of Siddha medicine from Lord Murukaṉ and also the guru of spiritual adepts called siddhas (cittar), who have eight supernatural powers. Prasad comments:

My guru is Agastiya, one of the eighteen siddhas. Today, the medium for Agastiya is Subramaniya, a farmer from the Nayakar (Vanniyar) community who lives in the village Walajahpet where our temple is. When renovating the temple, I was told by a gentleman in the village [[P144]] about Subramaniya and went to meet him. There is a group of about twelve people who follow this medium. I travel about 160 kilometres every month to visit him. However, I talk by phone every day with him. This helps me understand Hindu philosophy and the experiences I have with chanting mantras.

The Sattadas at Sriperumbudur

On the way from Chennai to Sriperumbudur to interview some Sattadas, K. K. A. Venkatachari gave me background information on this endogamous group, which does various jobs in the temple. ‘Sattadas go to ācāryas and jīyars for samāśrayaṇam,’ he said. ‘Today, my relative Koyil Andan Swami, for instance, does initiation for those at Sriperumbudur.’

Venkatachari remarked, ‘I remember one time when the priests brought water from the lake to the Sriperumbudur temple. They did this every morning. Each day, a Sattada followed the procession, entered the temple and prostrated himself before God. One day, he didn’t get up for some time. Then everyone realised that he had died.’ Venkatachari’s reminiscences quickly came to an end as we saw the gopurams of the Sriperumbudur temple on the horizon. Soon, we were at the home of a Sattada named Srinivasin. He narrated the following:

My family is the only Sattada family left in Sriperumbudur, although there are more in Kanchipuram. My grandfather, Narasimha, who died in 1990, was a Sattada at the Sriperumbudur temple. My father works in the temple, as do I. Devotees may put offerings on a plate, which comes to us. My other brother Raghavan works in the electrical business; his income is an important resource for supporting our family.

The traditional job of Sattadas is temple service. We prepare for the daily temple pūjās, which take place from 7–11 in the morning and 4.30–9 in the evening. We make the garlands, for instance. Only Sattadas make the [[P145]] garlands that have the image of a parrot created from leaves and flowers; these are the garlands associated with Āṇṭāḷ. Both men and women make garlands; only men offer them in the temple. Sattada women have no other specific religious jobs. In addition, we keep the temple keys, buy groceries for the temple, carry the images on a palanquin in processions, protect the deities’ ornaments, and keep crowds in order at festival times. During processions outside the temple, we protect the image and carry the torch. At Sriperumbudur, Sattadas have a separate trust that feeds Sattadas at festival times.

I interviewed Cakrapani, too, who belongs to another Sattada family. He began by recounting the history of the Sattadas, especially their role of protecting the temple images when they were about to be destroyed by the Muslims. ‘The Sattadas are equal to Brahmins,’ he said, ‘but do not wear the sacred thread, because when they have to do the worship five times a day, it is too much.’ He did not tell me why it is too much.

I’m a fifth-generation Sattada here. One hundred years ago, there were twenty-five Sattada families in Sriperumbudur. Others are at Srirangam, Srivilliputur, Alvartirunagari and Tirukkoyilur. Members of my family still do some service in the temple, although usually not in a formal sense. When they do service, they are paid for their time. Today, many Sattadas, including my own people, are acting as priests in Vaiṣṇava temples, mostly village ones.

Learning about the decline of the Sattada tradition in major temples, I asked him if this was a sign that Hinduism will die out.

It won’t die out. Hinduism survived through Muslim and British rule. The tradition will go on. We have our gurus. Of course, there will be some changes in our way of life. There were periodic reforms in the past and will be today as well. The 1,000-year anniversary of the birth of Kūreśa, Rāmānuja’s scribe, is this year. [[P146]]

Back in Chennai, I went to the home of Dr Rajagopalam, a Sattada and former schoolteacher, who had completed his Ph.D. at Bharathiar University in Coimbatore in 1993, and had retired in 1995. His desire for learning was inspired by his father who ran a school, was a scholar in Telugu and Tamil, and read many books. Rajagopalam was initiated by Varataa Ethiraja Jeeyar of Sriperumbudur and has memorised all 4,000 verses of the āḻvārs. He recites most often the Tiruppāvai, Tirupallāṇṭu, and Tirupaḷḷiyeḻucci. Here is his take on Sattada history:

Sattada is the name of our community in Tamil Nadu; Chāttāda is the name in Andhra. Sattada refers to those without the sacred thread and who are not registered at the temple for the right to recite the Divyaprabandham there. This was the result of a legal case at Srirangam about 100 years ago over the Sattadas’ right to recite the two lines of an āḻvār verse. The court took this right away from the Sattadas and gave it to the Brahmins. Because of lack of finances and will power, the Sattadas could not contest this, and so the Brahmins obtained the right of recitation for the entire verse [for more about this case, see Chapter 7].

However, Sattadas do chant for families in their homes during the ten-day ritual after death and at other private gatherings, and they sing wedding verses such as Periyāḻvār’s verse, ‘I have only one daughter’,66 and the sixth decade of Āṇṭāḷ’s Nācciyārtirumoḻi, which is about her dream of marrying Lord Raṅganātha. If they cannot chant because they have not memorised the verses for their own rites of passage, they hire a Śrīvaiṣṇava Brahmin to do so.

There are other differences between the Sattadas and Teṉkalai Brahmins. The old generation, my father’s generation, didn’t wear a dhoti like the Brahmins; they wore only a lungi. They did wear their hair in a tuft similar to that of the Brahmins. Although technically Sattadas are those who serve in the temple but don’t wear a sacred thread, some Sattadas wear it to do pūjās in village temples. People associate priests with sacred [[P147]] threads, and so they want their priest to wear one. Unlike the Brahmins who chant the gāyatrī mantra after their sacred thread ceremony, Sattadas don’t chant it. Neither do Sattadas study the Vedas, recite Vedic stotras or chant Vedic mantras.

The Academic Devotee and his Village

K. K. A. Venkatachari introduced me to Dr Parandhaman.

[[P148]] Knowing that I was interested in non-Brahmin Śrīvaiṣṇavas, Parandhanan started the interview by giving me several examples of Śrīvaiṣṇava caste inclusiveness. The first one is from the Tirukkottiyur sthala-purāṇa.

Service (kaiṅkarya) is good for everyone. We are born to do service in the world. God has given us body and soul to render service. Those who do so are Bhāgavatas.

Long ago other people, when they came to earth, forgot about God and his disciples. They had interest only in worldly life. When Lord Viṣṇu saw this, he became very sorry for them, and so he introduced the Vedas, Itihāsas, Purāṇas and Śāstras. They did not use these scriptures. Next, he gave the mantra om namo nārāyaṇāya, still to no avail. After that, he gave the essence of the Vedas in the Tirumantram, thinking that this would be easy. Because he gave this mantra at Badrinath in the Himalayas yet found no suitable people there to teach it, he himself became a teacher named Nārāyaṇa and also became a disciple named Nara.67 This, too, was not fruitful, and so he decided to be born on the earth as ten avatāras born in various castes.

This is the main thesis of Śrīvaiṣṇavism: caste inclusiveness shown by God himself. Even after incarnating as avatāras, God had not attained his goal. Next, he sent the nityasūris (eternal beings, in this case, the āḻvārs) to be born on earth and sing songs with the meanings of the Vedas. Here, too, there was no caste. Nammāḻvār was in the fourth varṇa (śūdra); Tiruppāṇ in the last (outcaste); and Periyāḻvār in the first (Brahmin). Tirumaṅkai was a thief. There was no difference.

Next, the nityasūris became twenty-four ācāryas to give the meanings of 4,000 hymns to the people. The first ācārya was Viṣṇu, the second his consort Mahālakṣmī, and so on up to Rāmānuja. Because the mantras had been kept secret, Rāmānuja wanted to learn them. With great difficulty, he went from Srirangam to Tirukkottiyur eighteen times to learn the meanings, and also fasted for forty days. Only then did the priest at Tirukkottiyur give him the meanings. After that, Rāmānuja thought, ‘How can I give these meanings to everyone?’ Therefore, he simplified the process, giving them all in public at the Tirukkottiyur temple and then asked the Brahmin ācāryas to teach them to everyone. As a cloud gives rain to all, so [[P149]] Rāmānuja gave knowledge and salvation to all. Because of these social reformers, Śrīvaiṣṇavism became popular.

Here is another story he told me:

When Rāmānuja brought the statue of Sampatkumar from Delhi, thieves attacked him. Harijans protected Rāmānuja and the statue. To thank the Harijans, he allowed them into the Lord’s temple at Melkote (a tradition that has continued to the modern period) and called them Tirukkulattār (members of the sacred family). Rāmānuja was the first reformer. After that, only Mahatma Gandhi has been a reformer.

Parandhaman then told two more anecdotes. The first was about a wrestler named Villidāsar, who worked as a guard for the Cōḻa king at his Uraiyur capital, next to Srirangam. The second story was about Rāmānuja’s belief in equality.

The wrestler met a beautiful girl. He fell in love and married her. In the summer, he took her with him to guard the king. Because they had to cross the hot sands of the dry Kaveri riverbed, he had his wife walk on his cloth and provided her shade with an umbrella. Because Indian men should not serve ladies, the people who witnessed his behaviour became agitated and told Rāmānuja. However, Rāmānuja understood the wrestler’s love and decided that his affection should be directed toward Lord Viṣṇu. Later, Rāmānuja took the wrestler and his wife to worship Lord Raṅganātha in the Srirangam temple. Lord Raṅganātha made them his disciples.68

By now, I had heard the last story many times. The conversation turned to Parandhaman’s own life:

The name of my village is Nariyampakkam; it is about 10 kilometres from Sriperumbudur. In the past, when we saw the temple gopuram, we would pray. My grandfather had eight sons and three daughters. Ramanuja Acarya, who is now Embar Jiyar, from Sriperumbudur, initiated my grandfather and my parents. Today, only some of their offspring have had samāśrayaṇam.

When I was a child, I walked 8 kilometres through a forest to a village school. I studied to the eighth grade there. Then I went by bike 15 kilometres to Tambaram to pre-university. I got married, and we had six children: four daughters and two sons. Five are now married. My wife, my children and I are all vegetarian; we cook vegetarian food and offer it to God. We live in Tambaram, although I often return to my village.

I became a clerk and then the superintendent and deputy registrar in Anna University. In this job, I was known for my efficient administration and integrity. Even on the day of my retirement farewell party, I still worked, giving out the benefit checks. I never accepted bribes for anything. I have also been doing academic degrees. I have done two M.A.s, an M.Phil. and a Ph.D. from the University of Madras with a thesis on the Nayakar (Vanniyar) caste. I wrote that in Tamil. Since retirement, I have been teaching Tamil.

In 1979, when I was forty-five, I was on pilgrimage, visiting temples and doing maṅgalāśāsana (chanting the verses about those divyadeśas). At Tirukkurunkudi, I needed a dhoti to enter the temple and didn’t have one. Tirunarayana Aiyankar, the Brahmin manager and priest, asked me where I was from. I told him near Sriperumbudur. He gave me a silk dhoti, so that I could enter the temple. He arranged tirumantiram teaching and gave me a book. This inspired me to have samāśrayaṇam in Nagerkoyil. I wanted fruitful gains; any religious act multiplies 100 times with initiation. Tirunarayana Aiyankar is still alive. I saw him last month and dedicated my book to him, which was published with special blessing songs at a book-release function.69

Now I live in Tambaram. Varataa Ethiraja Jeeyar70 once lived here. Before becoming a jīyar, he came to my house and recited prayers. He has walked 4,000 kilometres throughout Tamil Nadu. Working hard for Bhāgavatas, he founded the Tirumal Atiyar Kulam, of which I am now in charge. We celebrate the birthdays of the āḻvārs and ācāryas, feed Bhāgavatas, and hold processions on new-moon days. In addition, we hold functions in various places. Before starting one, we go through the streets to celebrate non-Brahmins singing in public. This breaks down the Brahmin monopoly on religion. One Brahmin told me not to sing at all in public. As we go in procession, we might stop at a temple, a tree or a sabhā building. Anywhere the event can occur. We sing bhajanas and then three or four Bhāgavatas, good orators, will speak about the āḻvārs in loud voices. Sometimes Vaiṣṇavas in a village invite us, pay for our bus fare and give prasādam. Those who give help might not wear the nāmam [that is, they may not even be Śrīvaiṣṇavas, or they are modern Śrīvaiṣṇavas who have given up this custom].71

Many topics were covered in my interviews in and around Chennai, from sectarian and personal religious beliefs and activities, to views of formal initiation, religious leadership, organisations with religious aspects (such as sabhās), caste (especially regarding Brahmins, but also other castes, including Dalits), and the relation of both religion and caste to politics. Because I found that many of these topics were covered in similar ways in my interviews from other regions of Tamil Nadu, I will save discussion of their similarities and differences for the book’s final chapter, and in this chapter and Chapters 3 and 4, I will conclude simply by noting some of the region’s highlights.

In the case of greater Chennai, several points stand out. One is the fact that conversations often mentioned Sriperumbudur, the birthplace of Rāmānuja, the key figure of Śrīvaiṣṇavism. In fact, in the area surrounding this temple, including nearby Kanchipuram, were born many key Śrīvaiṣṇava figures: besides Rāmānuja and his family, there were Tirumaḻicaiāḻvār, Tirukkaccinampi, Mutaliyāṇṭāṉ, Empār, and Kūreśa. I found [[P153]] that the people I interviewed from Chennai and beyond had far more connections with Sriperumbudur than with the famous temples in Kanchipuram. In part, this can be explained by the fact that the key organiser for my fieldwork was K. K. A. Venkatachari, whose ancestors go back to Rāmānuja’s nephew Mutaliyāṇṭāṉ. Venkatachari’s family has had a relationship with the Sriperumbudur temple over generations, including family members still living in Sriperumbudur. Many of the people I met had connections with this family.

There were other ācārya families who had their own networks of disciples. A number of maṭhas exist in Sriperumbudur, such as the Varada Yadugiri Yatiraja Maṭha72 and the maṭha founded by J. J. Thirunarayana Ramanuja Dasar for Dalits, which his granddaughter N. D. Sabari Natchi was renovating.73 The maṭhas all had their own networks of family members, disciples, or caste associates, which extended into the surrounding villages where the ācāryas and jīyars had proselytised.

This certainly explains some of the emphasis on Sriperumbudur that I encountered. Nevertheless, I think that this emphasis was also because of the disputes between Teṉkalais and Vaṭakalais in Kanchipuram at the Varadarajaswami temple. Moreover, the major Vaiṣṇava temple in Chennai, the Parthasarathy temple, has Vaikhānasa āgama priests, which occasionally has caused conflicts. At Sriperumbudur, by contrast, the Teṉkalai administrative control was aligned with the Pañcarātra āgama priesthood, and there were no major disputes with the Vaṭakalais, thereby providing a consolidated identity and therefore a hub for Teṉkalai networks in the Chennai region.

Another hub, albeit a remote one, for Teṉkalais has been Tirumalai-Tirupati. The history of this sacred place predates the formation of Śrīvaiṣṇavism as a sect. Today its fame as a pilgrimage destination attracts Teṉkalais from the region around Chennai, such as Sudarshana’s disciples (as it attracts other Śrīvaiṣṇavas and Hindus in general). The fact that [[P154]] Teṉkalai jīyars have special privileges and honours at these temples facilitates the Teṉkalai pilgrims and the larger Teṉkalai networks.

Finally, the fact that Chennai is the major metropolis of Tamil Nadu helps to explain other aspects of the interviews from this region. Many of my interviewees were first-generation or second-generation migrants from the immediate region, although some had come from places across Tamil Nadu for education and job opportunities. They were part of the phenomenon of urbanisation and the growing middle class.
