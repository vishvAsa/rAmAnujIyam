FOREWORD By Prof. M. Narasimhachary

Immense is my pleasure in writing this foreword to the Śrībhāṣya- bhāvacanḍrikā, a new Sanskrit commentary composed by Sri. U. Ve. P. M. Chakravarti Acharya Swami on the celebrated Śrībhāṣya of Śrī Rāmānuja. The revered Swami who is a well-known authority in Nyāya and Vedānta, among other sastras is an authentic and committed exponent of the Śrībhāṣya. His kālakṣepa-s on the Śrīvaiṣṇava traditional lore, now being held regularly at the Deśsika Bhavana (Mylapore, Chennai) are quite popular with the devout jijñāsu-āstikas.

The Śrībhāṣya, which is the monumental and masterful commentary of Rāmānuja on the Brahmasūtra-s of Bādarāyaṇa is an epoch-making work. Rāmānuja based it on the Bodhāyanavṛtti which was available to him in Kashmir. Although Nāthamuni and Yamuna, the great predecessors of Rāmānuja were known for their prodigious learning and argumentative skills, they did not comment upon it. Yāmuna in his Ātmasiddhi and Agamaprāmāṇya had indeed commented upon some aphorisms of Bādarāyaṇa (II.2.39 to 42; II.3.17 to 19) controverting the position of Sankara. Then why did he leave the Brahmasūtra without commenting upon it in its entirety? That reason in all probability could be the non-availability of the "Bodhāyanavṛtti" which was known to him by mere hearsay.

Yamuna perhaps thought that it would be ideal if the vṛtti of Bodhāyana was first gone through before attempting to comment upon the Brahmasutra. It was perhaps known to him to have existed somewhere in the North. He, due to his failing health and lack of manpower would not have ventured to go in search of the Bodhāyanavṛtti. Rāmānuja, however, who had established a strong base at Śrīrangam and who had enough organisational skills, made a trip to the North along with his trusted disciple, Kureśa, chanced to go through a rare manuscript of the Bodhāyana- vṛtti at Kashmir', commited it to memory, returned to Śrīrangam and wrote a full- fledged commentary on the Brahmasūtra. He thus fulfilled the unexpressed wish of his paramaguru, Yāmuna.

Rāmānuja, in the opening sentence of his Śrībhāṣya itself makes it clear that there existed indeed the elaborate vṛtti of Bhagavān Bodhāyana on the Brahmasūtra which was condensed by the Pūrvācārya-s, who, according to the Śrutaprakāśika were Dramiḍabhāṣyakāra and others. In his Vedārthasangraha Rāmānuja quotes Vākyakāra (Brahmanandin or Tanka) six times and Dramiḍabhāṣyakāra four times. In the Śrībhāṣya he quotes Bodhāyana six times and Vākyakāra and Dramiḍa- bhāṣyakāra four times each. All this is to show how Rāmānuja and his predecessors valued the tradition in understanding and interpreting the Brahmastra and the scriptural lore. Without a proper traditional background, one will not only miss the basic teaching of a given text but also mislead others through wrong interpretation.

For instance, in the light of Yamuna's Gītārthasangraha, we are in a position to understand the the teaching of the Gītā in all its details - its central teaching, the background against which the Gītā has come as a sastra, its division into three hexads (saṭkas), the topics of each chapter and the quintessential teaching of the text. If we do not know the plan of the work as understood by our Pūrvācāryas, we will miss the point. Śrī Vedānta Deśika, while explaining the last part of the last line of the Gitārtha- sangraha, takes occasion to show that this compendium of Yamuna has behind it, a very ancient and reliable tradition. This is true with almost all the texts that have come down to us from time immemorial.

The interpretation of the Prasthānatraya should therefore be backed by a strong and reliable tradition. Since each Acārya who has commented upon the triple texts lays claim to his own traditional background, we should look for a logical basis that governs all such claims. In so far as Rāmānuja is concerned he based his Śrībhāṣya on the vṛtti of Bodhāyana and the works of other Pūrvācārya-s like Brahmanandin and Dramiḍa.

Although we are not in a position to say more about the Bodhāyanavṛtti, on the authority of Mahācārya, the author of Śrībhāṣyopanyāsa, we come to know that Bodhāyana's vṛtti reflects the heart of Bādarāyaṇa since he was separated from the sūtrakāra just by one teacher. The longer the gap between a given writer and his commentator, the larger will be scope of missing the intended traditional interpretation. This may be clarified by citing one illustration. Śrī Śankara interpreted the Utpattyasambhava section of the Brahmasutra (II.2.39 to 41) in such a way that all the four aphorisms contradict the validity of Pāñcarātra Āgamas.

While he agrees with the Pāñcarātra view that Bhagavān Vāsudeva grants liberation to those who worship him through right knowledge, steadfast contemplation and so on, he is not able to accept the theory of the "origination" (utpatti) of the Jiva (Sankarṣaṇa), Manas (Pradyumna) and ahaṁkära (Aniruddha) of which, each succeeding Tattva is derived from the previous one. "How can a Jiva be said to be born?" he asks in all seriousness.

The reply given by Yamuna in his Āgamaprāmāṇya is also equally serious: "it is true. Jīva is not born. As a matter of fact, the Pāñcarātra Āgama-s themselves do declare that a jiva is not subject to birth and death, and that he is eternal. The terms jīva, manas and ahaṁkāra do not mean the individual self, mind and ego in the normal sense. They are used in a technical sense. They refer to the "vyuha"-s or emanations that the Lord assumes for facilitating the worship of people belonging to all the four classes." It is thus clear that Śrī Sankara's criticism of the Pañcarātra- vyūha theory is not based upon a proper understanding of the Pañcarātra tradition and is thus beside the point.

That is why, the great Advaitin Amalānandasarasvati who appeared a few centuries after Sankara and who knew the Pāñcarātra tradition had to explain the utpattyasambhava section in his Kalpataruparimala in such a way that the reference to the so called "origin" of the jiva found in the Āgama-s need not be taken primarily and literally. Is not Sankara's interpretation of the famous Chandogya passage (1.6.7) "tasya yatha kapyāsam pundarikam evam akṣiņi" still an insurmountable puzzle even to his own followers?

It is thus imperative that if one misses the tradition, one is bound to blunder and flounder even in simple matters and much more so in the interpretation of serious texts like the prasthanatraya. We thus have fortunately with us, an authentic and authoritative commentary of Brahmasūtra-s in the form of the Śrībhāṣya which is founded on an excellent and unbroken tradition of Pūrvācārya-s. That Bodhāyana's vṛtti truly reflects the heart of Bādarāyaṇa and that it was "vaiṣṇava” in character follow without a question.

Now the present publication consisting of the lucid and learned commentary Śrībhāṣyabhāvacandrikā of Sri. U.Ve. Chakravarthi Acārya Swami covers upto the "vaiśvānarādhikaraṇa" (1.2.33) of the Śrībhāṣya. This commentary, it must be pointed out, is an outstanding exposition of the erudite Swami, which takes into its fold, the

merits of all the earlier commentaries that have appeared from time to time, both from the earlier ācārya-s as well as the recent preceptors. Rāmānuja, we understand, attached great importance to the proper understanding of his magnum opus, the Śrībhāṣya. This is clear from the fact that he appointed a few scholars as the Śrībhāṣya-simhāsanādhipati-s, to see that his valuable commentary on the Brahmasutra is understood and interpreted in the "traditional" way.

Sri.U.Ve. Chakravarthi Acarya Swami, I am sure, is our modern Śrībhāṣya- simhāsanādhipati. He combines in himself a vast experience of learning and expounding the vast ubhayavedānta lore in the traditional way. He has taught and is teaching devout disciples the Śrībhāṣya several times. Since the disciples are not necessarily scholars, he has made the original as simple as possible. It has to be pointed out in this connection that this masterpiece of Rāmānuja is by no means an easy text. It is the despair of those who have no grounding in Sanskrit language, in śāstra-s like Nyāya, Vaiseṣika, Mīmāṁsā and Vyākaraṇa and in the Āgama literature.

That our revered Acārya Swami has in his own unique and amazing way, made the work of Rāmānuja simple enough for the lay man, speaks volumes about his expository skills and methodical perfection. I take this oppurtunity to convey my cordial and most respectful praṇama-s to this noble soul living in our midst for taking the entire world of jijñāsu-s to the richest treasure we can ever think of – viz., the Śrībhāṣya in as effortless and easy a manner as possible. We are indeed beholden to this modern commentator who has made the understanding of the formidable Śrībhāṣya quite simple, without giving up the flavour of the tradition.

I only wish and pray that the "Emperor of Ascetics" (yati-cakrvartin) Śrī. Rāmānuja showers his chiocest blessings on this "Emperor of Commentators" (Cakravarthi Acarya) enabling him to bring out the subsequent volume of his Candrikā also in a shorter span of time for the benefit of scholars and laymen alike.
